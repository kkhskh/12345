# SECTION 1 — THE NONNEGOTIABLE RESEARCH QUESTION

Consider a frozen Transformer:

\[
F_\theta(x).
\]

Let candidate c have a defined model-decision outcome, such as a logit margin:

\[
m_c(x)=L_c(x)-L_{\mathrm{comp}}(x).
\]

For an actual internal intervention I, define:

\[
R_c(x,I)
=
m_c(F_\theta(x;I))-m_c(F_\theta(x)).
\]

Suppose the researcher proposes a candidate-specific consistency-related quantity:

\[
C_c(x).
\]

The central question is:

**Can a computationally grounded and independently identified consistency relationship imply a nontrivial, falsifiable restriction on the model's candidate-specific decision or causal decision response?**

The relationship may be a scalar, vector, operator, structural constraint, or another well-defined object.

But it must have genuine computational meaning.

A successful answer must establish a logically connected chain:

**Actual model computation**

→ **independently meaningful consistency relation**

→ **identified causal or predictive role**

→ **mathematically justified decision consequence**

→ **distinctive falsifiable prediction**.

Every arrow requires justification.

If the chain breaks, identify exactly where.

Do not patch a missing arrow by assuming the conclusion.

# SECTION 2 — WHAT WOULD ACTUALLY COUNT AS A CAUSAL BRIDGE?

There are at least three scientifically different possibilities.

## 2.1 Explicit implemented mechanism

The Transformer may contain a computational operation that responds to a relationship between internal variables.

Examples to investigate:

- A downstream component that responds to opposing candidate evidence.
- A gating operation whose behavior depends on a relation between two pathways.
- A correction process that suppresses certain conflicting contributions.
- A reconciliation operation that combines different candidate representations.
- A mechanism that changes the influence of one pathway conditional on another.

These are hypotheses, not established facts.

For this route, the research must identify:

1. The actual computational variables.
2. The operation implementing the relation.
3. The structural equations.
4. The intervention that changes its inputs.
5. The downstream causal pathway.
6. The resulting candidate-decision consequence.

A researcher-defined discrepancy score is not evidence that the model computes or responds to that score.

If the mechanism does not exist in the analyzed computation, do not claim that it does.

## 2.2 Computation-grounded causal predictor

A consistency-related statistic might predict intervention responses even if the model does not explicitly compute the statistic.

This is a weaker but potentially legitimate scientific claim.

For this route, establish:

- How the predictor is identified.
- What information it uses.
- Which intervention family it covers.
- What decision consequence it predicts.
- Why the prediction follows from model structure.
- Whether simpler predictors already imply the same result.

Do not describe the statistic as a physical causal mediator without evidence.

## 2.3 Necessary-condition or impossibility theory

It may turn out that consistency alone cannot determine a decision response without additional information.

A serious theoretical result could identify what information is necessary.

For example, an exact sign predictor based on a statistic must distinguish interventions with different response signs on its allowed domain.

But a necessary-condition theorem is not automatically a surviving causal mechanism.

It tells us what a mechanism would require.

It does not show that the Transformer contains one.

**Keep these three outcomes separate throughout the investigation.**

# SECTION 4 — RESOLVE THE EXACT-COMPUTATION PARADOX

This is one of the deepest unresolved issues.

Suppose a compatibility equation accurately represents the model's true computation:

\[
h_v=f_v(h_{\mathrm{parents}(v)}).
\]

A correctly recomputed forward pass satisfies it.

Correct and incorrect decisions may both arise from valid computations.

Therefore exact structural compatibility does not automatically determine decision correctness.

Now suppose we use a projected approximation:

\[
P_vh_v\approx \rho(P_uh_u).
\]

The discrepancy may represent lost information or approximation error.

It does not automatically represent decision conflict.

**The investigation must determine what notion of consistency could escape this dilemma without becoming circular.**

Explore, but do not assume the validity of:

### A. Consistency between computationally represented claims

Do different pathways encode competing candidate-related content?

How would those claims be independently identified?

Does the downstream computation actually compare them?

### B. Consistency under a causal abstraction

Do local representations satisfy relationships that preserve specified intervention effects?

Is the discrepancy an abstraction-faithfulness error rather than a decision obstruction?

### C. Consistency of counterfactual commitments

Does the model behave coherently under a defined family of related interventions?

Can that coherence predict candidate decisions without being defined using those decisions?

### D. Consistency of a computational reconciliation mechanism

Is there a real operation that transforms conflicting input signals into a selected candidate response?

Can the operation be identified independently?

### E. Consistency among partial sufficient representations

Do independently constrained internal representations jointly preserve a decision-relevant variable?

Is the proposed consistency relation necessary for that preservation?

For every version, answer:

**What exactly is consistent with what?**

**Who defines that relation?**

**Does the model use it?**

**What happens when the relation fails?**

**Why should that failure affect a candidate decision?**

**Can the relation hold for a wrong decision?**

**Can it fail for a correct decision?**

If the answer to the last two questions is yes, identify what additional assumptions are needed.

Do not hide this issue in a graph-theory appendix.

# SECTION 7 — A REAL CAUSAL IDENTIFICATION STANDARD

For any proposed consistency mechanism, specify its causal interpretation.

Is consistency:

1. An actual variable computed by the network?
2. A proxy for an internal operation?
3. A property of a representation?
4. A predictor of intervention response?
5. A constraint on a family of computations?
6. A researcher-defined post hoc statistic?

These roles differ.

If C is a deterministic function of hidden states, simply writing:

\[
\operatorname{do}(C=c)
\]

does not define a unique intervention on the Transformer.

Multiple state changes can produce the same C while affecting other causally relevant information differently.

You must define the actual structural intervention.

A claim that "consistency causes a decision improvement" requires a justified intervention semantics or an identified mechanism through which that property matters.

Where that cannot be justified, restrict the claim to causal-response prediction.

Do not confuse an intervention that changes C with an intervention on C itself.
