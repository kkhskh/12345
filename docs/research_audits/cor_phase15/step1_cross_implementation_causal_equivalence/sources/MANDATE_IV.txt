### Established results

**P-count pretraining:**

- Transfers successfully in 5/5 tested seeds.
- T4 frozen-feature transfer accuracy approximately 0.704–0.725.
- Stores leave-question agreement-count information at readout tokens.
- A count-based abstract-variable intervention has IIA approximately 0.60–0.72.

- Transfers successfully in 5/5 tested parents.

The previous work did not establish a causal equivalence between them.

The earlier claim that T1 carries a non-count variable was a probe-specification artefact and has been retracted.

The newer claim that both curricula carry a count variable is supported, but **identity of their causal variable remains unproved**.

This is your research target.

# 2. THE SINGLE CENTRAL QUESTION

Let \(M_P\) be a Transformer pretrained on explicit agreement counting and transferred to T4.

Let \(M_T\) be a Transformer pretrained on T1 and transferred to T4.

Let \(R(x)\) denote the relevant generator-defined agreement-count information.

The central question is:

**Is there an independently identifiable abstract relational state \(R\), possibly distributed across different token sites, whose controlled changes induce equivalent candidate-relevant computations in both \(M_P\) and \(M_T\)?**

You must determine what "equivalent" means mathematically.

Do not define it merely as:

- Both models decode agreement counts.
- Both models achieve good T4 accuracy.
- Both models approximate Bayes.
- Both models respond to changing source agreement.
- Both models have correlated subspaces.
- Both models pass separate probes.

Those are insufficient.

The proposed equivalence must make a nontrivial claim about causal computation.

# 3. FORMULATE COMPETING HYPOTHESES

Before conducting new confirmation experiments, define and freeze these hypotheses or stronger, more precise alternatives.

### H1 — Shared abstract causal variable

Both models implement a common decision-relevant relational variable, possibly at different sites and in different coordinate systems.

A transformation between the representations preserves the variable's relevant causal effects under a specified intervention family.

### H2 — Different projections of a larger relational state

P-count represents a global agreement-count vector.

T1 distributes separate group-count components across source tokens.

The representations overlap in information and causal influence but are not interchangeable without reconstructing additional state.

### H3 — Different computations with similar decision behavior

Both models use count-related information, but they perform different downstream operations.

They may agree because both approximate the same Bayesian decision function, rather than because they share an intermediate causal computation.

### H4 — Intervention-induced apparent equivalence

The identified count directions steer the output under artificial activation changes but do not isolate the naturally used computation.

High interchange accuracy may partly reflect:

- Encoder capacity.
- Representation correlations.
- Mismatched-counterfactual floors.
- LayerNorm effects.
- Off-manifold interventions.
- Overlapping or redundant pathways.

These hypotheses must produce distinguishable predictions.

Do not simply rank them by verbal plausibility.

# 4. FIRST MATHEMATICAL TASK: DEFINE CAUSAL EQUIVALENCE

Before training new models, formulate the strongest meaningful equivalence relation between two learned computations.

A promising starting point is a pair of abstractions:

\[
\alpha_P : Z_P \rightarrow V,
\qquad
\alpha_T : Z_T \rightarrow V,
\]

where \(Z_P\) and \(Z_T\) are appropriate internal computational states and \(V\) is a generator-defined relational variable or structured family of variables.

But a shared decoded \(V\) is not enough.

Develop a criterion requiring the following diagram to commute, exactly or approximately, for a specified family of valid interventions:

1. Intervene on the generator-defined relationship.
2. Observe the resulting model-specific computation.
3. Map the internal states into the proposed abstract variable.
4. Compare the abstract causal transitions and candidate-specific responses.

Distinguish at least:

**Information equivalence:** both representations contain the same relational information.

**Intervention equivalence:** changes to the abstract relational variable reproduce corresponding internal effects.

**Decision-response equivalence:** the causal effects on candidate margins agree within a specified tolerance.

**Full computational equivalence:** a stronger claim about the relevant downstream operations, not just the final output.

It is possible that only one or two of these hold.

Define the objects, quantifiers, domains, and tolerances precisely.

Specify what happens when the candidate's own evidence is held fixed.

Develop exact or approximate invariance conditions under independently fitted coordinate transformations.

Do not assume a globally invertible coordinate map exists.

Investigate whether a distributed representation requires a product or tuple of local abstractions rather than one centralized latent vector.

### Theoretical deliverable

A formal proposition, theorem, or impossibility result answering:

**Under what conditions can causally equivalent relational variables exist at different token sites, and what experimental evidence would identify that equivalence?**

If the proposed conditions are not identifiable from the available observations, prove or construct that limitation.

Do not manufacture an equivalence by definition.

# 5. SECOND TASK: IDENTIFY THE EXACT INFORMATION IN EACH IMPLEMENTATION

The previous results contain a crucial ambiguity.

P-count appears to store a leave-question count vector at readout tokens.

T1 may store the count of each token's own source group in separate source-token directions.

You must distinguish these possibilities experimentally.

### 5.1 Compare candidate abstract variables

At minimum, investigate:

- Full leave-question group-count vector.
- A token's own-group count.
- The collection of all group-specific counts across tokens.
- A reliability-posterior representation derived from those counts.
- A smaller decision-sufficient statistic, if one exists under the exact generator.
- Any necessary candidate- or question-conditioned version.

Define every variable from the generator, independently of model outcomes.

Determine mathematically whether the variables are informationally equivalent, strictly ordered by sufficiency, or incomparable.

Do not assume that having all local counts at separate sites is the same computational architecture as having the whole vector at one site.

### 5.2 Build discriminating counterfactual episodes

Construct valid input pairs where the competing representations predict different effects.

Examples include:

- Change one group's count while holding other group counts fixed.
- Change another group's count while preserving the first.
- Change the full count vector while keeping the candidate's own reports fixed.
- Preserve all counts while changing source identities or contents.
- Change source reports without changing the posterior-relevant statistic.
- Construct cases where a local group-count representation is insufficient but the full vector remains sufficient.

Use exact generator support checks.

Distinguish:

- Marginally supported episodes.
- Episodes possible under the same latent world.
- Input-level interventions corresponding to specified structural changes.
- Internal activation edits.

Where an intended intervention is impossible under the generator, do not silently substitute an off-support example.

### 5.3 Identify natural conditional dependence

Determine whether source-token states depend selectively on their own group's count or also on other groups' counts.

Control for source identity, token position, question index, source contents, and correlated generator variables.

Use independent held-out evaluation.

Include baseline probes with matched numbers of parameters.

For the T1 models, compare per-slot encoders with shared encoders, properly accounting for their different capacities.

For P-count models, investigate whether their readout representation is genuinely global or merely a sum of separately accessible group-specific components.

**Deliverable:** a precise account of the information structure implemented by each curriculum, including where existing evidence cannot distinguish the alternatives.

# 6. THIRD TASK: CAUSAL INTERCHANGE ACROSS THE TWO IMPLEMENTATIONS

This is the main experiment.

Do not treat decoding agreement as causal equivalence.

### 6.1 Establish within-model causal baselines

Reproduce the existing count-interchange tests for:

- Multiple P-count seeds.
- Multiple independent T1 parents.
- The relevant sites in each model.

Use the original counterfactual episode construction and add the discriminating intervention families from Section 5.

Measure:

- Candidate-margin change.
- Sign agreement with the counterfactual.
- Interchange intervention accuracy.
- Difference from full-site patching.
- Difference from generator-level source edits.
- Difference from simpler behavioral references.

Use informative pairs selected by frozen mathematical criteria.

Report unconditional results as well as selected-pair results.

Do not compare raw IIA scores with different chance floors without adjusting for those floors.

### 6.2 Construct independent abstract encoders

Fit mappings from generator-defined relational variables to each model's natural representation.

Fit these using natural training episodes and independently defined relationship labels.

Do not fit them to maximize candidate-margin agreement on the final intervention set.

Consider:

- Shared linear encoders.
- Per-slot linear encoders.
- Explicit distributed encoders.
- Low-complexity nonlinear encoders only where needed and justified.

Match the capacities of null encoders and alternative-feature encoders.

Freeze all mappings before final testing.

### 6.3 Test cross-model causal transport

A raw activation from one trained model generally cannot be inserted into another model and interpreted meaningfully.

Therefore do not simply copy a hidden vector between networks.

Instead, test whether a generator-defined relational intervention observed or identified in one model can be transported through independently fitted mappings into the recipient model's implementation.

For example:

- Identify the relational change in the donor.
- Translate it through the candidate abstract state.
- Implement the corresponding change at the recipient's independently identified sites.
- Recompute the recipient's downstream computation.
- Compare the resulting candidate-margin response with the recipient's natural counterfactual response.

Repeat with donor and recipient reversed.

The transformation must not be fitted on the held-out target responses.

Compare full-vector transport against local-component transport.

Investigate whether a distributed T1 state can be transported only by jointly changing multiple source-token sites.

### 6.4 Distinguish equivalence from shared Bayes approximation

Both models already approximate the same Bayesian decision rule.

Therefore matching final candidate decisions is a weak test.

Include controls where:

- Both models agree behaviorally but internal interventions predict different responses.
- Both use the same count information but different downstream transformations.
- A count-preserving source change affects the model despite leaving exact Bayes unchanged.
- A synthetic or engineered aggregate-only computation approximates the same Bayesian rule.
- A matched-capacity encoder transfers an incorrect variable.

The result must identify something beyond common output accuracy.

If only the output function agrees, state that explicitly.

### 6.5 Assess off-manifold effects

A valid counterfactual source episode does not guarantee that an internal representation patch lies on the recipient model's activation manifold.

Quantify:

- Representation norms.
- Covariance and conditional residuals.
- Model-specific activation distributions.
- Effects of partial versus full-state replacement.
- Sensitivity to normalization.
- Effects of alternative patching operators.

Use targeted source-level interventions as the natural reference wherever possible.

Do not claim that a cross-model abstract-variable patch is a natural intervention.

**Required result:** a direct test of whether the two curricular implementations are causally equivalent under a specified, nontrivial family of relational interventions.

A demonstrated failure is as scientifically valuable as a success.

# 7. FOURTH TASK: ESTABLISH NECESSITY WITHOUT COLLATERAL DAMAGE

The current mean-ablation results are suggestive but not decisive.

Some reported effects exceed the size of the relational performance advantage itself.

That indicates possible collateral damage.

Investigate whether the count-related variable is necessary for the natural relational benefit.

Use:

- Per-position control statistics.
- Conditional rather than unconditional replacement where possible.
- Matched-variance subspaces.
- Wrong-group-count controls.
- Count-preserving source-edit controls.
- Orthogonal and overlapping component controls.
- Restoration after ablation.
- Redundant-path tests.

Do not use an intervention that changes many unrelated computational variables as evidence that a specific count variable is necessary.

If the model has redundant relational pathways, distinguish:

- Individual component necessity.
- Joint necessity.
- Causal contribution.
- Replaceability.
- Natural computational use.

Investigate whether removing the proposed abstract variable specifically moves the model toward the own-question posterior rather than merely producing arbitrary damage.

The outcome must be compared against the corresponding candidate-specific Bayesian reference.

### Important

A successful restoration experiment should restore the lost relational response, not simply improve generic accuracy.

A failed ablation effect does not establish non-use if the variable is redundant.

A high interchange score does not establish unique mediation.

# 8. FIFTH TASK: WHY DOES THE VARIABLE MOVE BETWEEN TOKEN SITES?

Only after Sections 4–7 have established a suitable characterization should you investigate this question.

The previous atlas suggested:

- P-count's direct counting objective encourages a readout-token representation.
- T1's answer-supervised objective encourages source-token reliability information.

That explanation is plausible but untested.

Construct a small, matched intervention on training objectives.

For example:

- Train the same counting objective with supervision applied at a source token.
- Train an otherwise matched model with supervision applied at a readout token.
- Compare resulting feature locations and downstream transfer.
- Include controls for attention masks, number of supervised positions, gradient magnitude, and total optimization budget.

The key prediction is not simply that a probe follows the supervised site.

It is whether **the site of relational computation and its downstream causal pathway change predictably with the objective while preserving, or altering, the abstract decision-relevant computation**.

Use multiple seeds if feasible.

If compute is insufficient, prioritize the existing-model causal equivalence tests over new training.

Do not turn this into a broad curriculum sweep.

# 13. WHAT COUNTS AS A SUBSTANTIVE RESULT?

The central investigation can have three scientifically meaningful outcomes.

### Outcome A — A shared causal relational variable is supported

You identify a precisely defined abstract state and demonstrate that different representations instantiate the same relevant causal transition and candidate-decision response.

The result survives strong null controls and multiple independent models.

This would establish a restricted, implementation-independent causal abstraction for this specific relational computation.

It would not prove the whole COR theory.

### Outcome B — The models share only a partial abstraction

For example, they encode the same agreement statistics, but the distributed T1 implementation and centralized P-count implementation differ in downstream causal operations or in which components are sufficient.

You characterize the shared part and the nonshared part mathematically.

This would be a valuable classification result.

### Outcome C — The proposed equivalence fails

Both models use relational information but cannot be identified as causally equivalent under the justified intervention family.

You provide a concrete counterexample or controlled experimental separation.

This would show why simple common-variable accounts are insufficient even within one relational task.

That negative result would help define the boundaries of future theories.
