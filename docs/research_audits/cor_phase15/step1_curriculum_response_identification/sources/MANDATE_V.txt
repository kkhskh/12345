### Established mathematical setting

\[
V=n^{(-q)}
\]

where V contains the leave-question agreement counts for the source groups.

# 3. THE CENTRAL COMPETING HYPOTHESES

T1 and P-count pretraining induce systematically different operations that transform agreement-count information into candidate evidence.

- Bayesian posterior approximation error.
- Output calibration.
- Model architecture.

## H2 — Shared computation with curriculum-dependent approximation errors

## H3 — Training-parent or seed dependence

## H4 — Representation or intervention artifact

- Incomplete count decoding.
- Correlation with own-question evidence.
- The choice of activation site.
- Activation-addition artifacts.
- Normalization.
- Inconsistent distributed representations.
- The selected response metric.
- Differences in output scale.

Natural input-level responses must be compared with surgical internal interventions.

## H5 — A common abstract operation with different nuisance pathways

Both curricula may implement the same core count-dependent inference operation while differing in auxiliary information, redundant pathways, or irrelevant residual computations.

Their responses to π-preserving changes may differ even if their decision-relevant core is equivalent.

This is distinct from both a fully shared algorithm and fundamentally different relational decision algorithms.

# 4. FIRST MATHEMATICAL TASK: CORRECT THE DECISION-EQUIVALENCE THEOREM

Investigation IV's Theorem C3 needs a precise qualification.

It states, approximately, that two exactly Bayes-optimal models with exact abstract intervention equivalence have identical decision-response functions on natural support.

This is false if "Bayes-optimal" means only optimal classification accuracy.

For example, a model producing logits proportional to log posterior probabilities and another producing twice those logits can have identical Bayes-optimal argmax decisions but different logit changes.

Therefore, before investigating the learned-model differences:

### 4.1 Define four distinct equivalences

**Classification equivalence:** models choose the same Bayes-optimal candidate.

**Posterior equivalence:** models return the same calibrated Bayesian candidate probabilities.

**Log-odds equivalence:** models return equal candidate log-probability ratios under a fixed convention.

**Computational equivalence:** models implement equivalent downstream causal operations under a declared intervention family.

Do not conflate these.

### 4.2 Prove the appropriate restricted theorem

Determine under which assumptions:

\[
\Delta m_M
=
\Delta m_{M'}
\]

must hold for two models.

Specify:

- Whether m is a logit difference or calibrated log posterior odds.
- What exact Bayes matching means.
- Which intervention pairs lie on natural support.
- Whether the abstract intervention matches the natural counterfactual pointwise.
- Whether equality holds for every episode or only on average.
- The relevant gauge or score normalization.

Derive the approximate version.

For example, show how pointwise approximation errors bound differences between the models' candidate-margin responses.

Then construct explicit counterexamples demonstrating why weaker notions of Bayes-optimality are insufficient.

### 4.3 Distinguish exact posterior invariance from learned-model invariance

For π-preserving count changes, the exact Bayesian probability response is zero.

Investigate what can be said about learned models that approximate the posterior in:

- KL divergence.
- Total variation.
- Pointwise probability error.
- Log-odds error.
- Classification accuracy.

Show which of these can and cannot bound π-preserving response errors without further assumptions.

### Required output

A corrected theorem and counterexample package that separates posterior correctness, optimal candidate selection, and downstream computational equivalence.

Do not claim a new theorem merely by restating an elementary approximation inequality.
