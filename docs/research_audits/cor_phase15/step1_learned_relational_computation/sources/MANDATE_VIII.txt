# 1. THE EXACT STARTING POINT

Investigations VI and VII established a prospective decision failure and a subsequent behavioral repair.

In the T4 latent-source-reliability environment, P-count-pretrained Transformers frequently make the wrong candidate update when a group reaches perfect agreement in a specific configuration.

The relevant configuration, R1, occurs when:

- One group is already perfect on the other questions.
- Another group changes from 4/5 agreement to 5/5.
- The newly perfect group supports the same candidate as the existing perfect group.
- Exact Bayes requires a positive candidate-evidence update.

Previously confirmed mean responses were approximately:

- Exact Bayes: +0.23.
- T1-pretrained models: +0.19.
- P-count-pretrained models: −0.51.

The sign difference replicated prospectively across eight independent models in each curriculum.

Investigation VII then identified an exact generator-derived eligibility factor:

\[
m_g =
\sum_{h\neq g}
\mathbf{1}[n_h=5]\,
\mathbf{1}[r_h(q)=r_g(q)].
\]

Here:

- \(n_h\) is group h's leave-question agreement count.
- \(r_h(q)\) is its report on the target question.
- \(m_g\) measures the number of other perfect groups agreeing with group g's target-question report.

In the restricted exact generator, candidate evidence follows a mixture expression of the form:

\[
p_B(y\mid X)
\propto
\sum_g
\rho_*^{\,n_g}
m_g
L_g(y).
\]

The original documents define \(\rho_*\) and \(L_g\) precisely.

Use the original definitions rather than assuming that this expression is valid outside its specified generator conditions.

The important mathematical finding was that a restricted mixture-form decision rule depending only on each group's own count cannot reproduce the correct R1/R2/R3 response-sign pattern.

It needs information about other groups.

## The Investigation VII rescue

Claude supplied the exact generator-derived feature \(m_g\) to the downstream model and trained eight new P-count lineages under a frozen protocol.

The result:

| Measurement | Original | With \(m_g\) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Mean signed R1 excess | −0.724 | −0.029 |
| Mean absolute R1 excess | 0.724 | 0.302 |
| Mean absolute R2/R3 excess | 1.228 | 0.514 |
| Overall KL to Bayes | 0.0704 | 0.0386 |
| Accuracy | 0.725 | 0.731 |

The R1 response improved in eight out of eight independently trained models.

Seven of eight passed the frozen preservation criteria.

But only five of eight produced the correct R1 response sign.

The repair did not identify a unique native neural mechanism.

And \(m_g\) was supplied by a hand-computed function of the input.

### Crucial interpretation

The repair demonstrates that providing the relevant relational conjunction helps the learned decision.

It does **not** demonstrate that the original Transformer learned to compute that conjunction internally.

It also does not prove that lack of this exact feature is the unique cause of the original failure.

That gap is the focus of Investigation VIII.

# 3. THE RESEARCH PROGRAM

Investigation VIII has six interconnected scientific problems.

These are not six unrelated projects.

They jointly address how the relational evidence-update operation is acquired and used.

## Problem A — Learnability

Can a Transformer construct the eligibility computation from raw available evidence, without having \(m_g\) explicitly calculated for it?

## Problem B — Accessibility

Are the required inputs present in frozen representations in a form that the downstream architecture can combine?

What is the distinction between statistical information and computationally accessible information?

## Problem C — Optimization

Why does the original transfer process favor the wrong R1 response?

What changes in training, data distribution, capacity, or objective allow the correct conditional computation to emerge?

## Problem D — Neural implementation

When a model successfully learns the relevant relation, what does its actual computation look like?

Does it use a source-token route, a readout-token route, attention-mediated group comparison, an MLP conjunction, or a different distributed implementation?

## Problem E — Causal sufficiency and necessity

Does the learned internal operation actually mediate the correct candidate decision?

Can it be selectively changed without damaging other evidence interpretations?

## Problem F — Generalization and mathematical structure

Does the operation capture a reusable relationship among evidence sources, or only a shortcut adapted to one generator configuration?

Can we formulate restricted mathematical results explaining when the operation is learnable, identifiable, and decision-relevant?

A successful investigation should advance several of these problems and be explicit about the ones that remain unresolved.

# 4. FIRST MATHEMATICAL TASK: DEFINE THE RELATIONAL COMPUTATION PRECISELY

Before constructing new models, establish what must be computed.

Let the input provide:

- Source reports across questions.
- Source and group identities.
- Agreement counts or information from which they can be calculated.
- Current-question candidate evidence.

Define the eligibility computation:

\[
m_g =
\sum_{h\ne g}
I(n_h=5)\,
I(r_h(q)=r_g(q)).
\]

The function combines three operations:

1. Identifying perfect groups.
2. Comparing their target-question reports.
3. Aggregating eligible cross-group agreements.

These operations may be realized in different computational architectures.

Do not assume a Transformer must explicitly store a variable named \(m_g\).

It may instead compute an equivalent sufficient statistic, a different factorization, or a direct candidate-conditioned mapping.

## 4.1 Formalize functional equivalence

Define when a learned computation \(f_\theta(X)\) is equivalent to the eligibility operation.

Distinguish:

- Exact equality to \(m_g\).
- Equality up to an invertible representation.
- Equality of downstream Bayesian candidate responses.
- Equality only on observed training support.
- Equality on held-out source configurations.
- Approximate equivalence under a specified error metric.

A model that predicts the same candidate choices on the training distribution is not automatically computing \(m_g\).

## 4.2 Define candidate-response equivalence

For a candidate pair \((c,c')\):

\[
\Delta m_M =
m_M(x')-m_M(x).
\]

Compare with exact Bayes:

\[
E_M=\Delta m_M-\Delta m_B.
\]

Use candidate log-probability ratios and probability-space responses as distinct measures.

Do not infer an identical computation merely because candidate rankings match.

Use proper probability scoring rules, especially for tied and near-tied cases.

## 4.3 Identify minimal relational requirements

Determine which aspects of the raw input are mathematically necessary for the R1/R2/R3 response pattern.

Examine whether the computation requires:

- Every group count.
- Only the set of perfect groups.
- Target-question votes.
- Equality relationships between votes.
- Source-group identities.
- The current candidate being evaluated.
- An explicit posterior over latent source roles.

Derive lower bounds on the information needed by restricted decision-rule families.

If there are multiple sufficient representations, characterize their equivalence class.

Avoid declaring \(m_g\) universally minimal without proof.

## 4.4 Stronger theorem targets

Seek restricted results of the form:

- A separable groupwise evidence rule cannot realize the required response-sign pattern.
- A specified class of pairwise relational operations can realize it.
- Certain downstream architectural restrictions prevent the required conjunction.
- Under explicit conditions, a candidate-conditioned relational factor guarantees the correct sign.
- The same count representation can support either correct or incorrect candidate updates depending on its downstream computation.

Provide assumptions and counterexamples.

Do not label elementary identities new COR theorems.

# 5. SECOND TASK: THE DECISIVE LEARNED-COMPOSITION EXPERIMENT

The most important experiment compares hand-computed \(m_g\) with learned computations that must construct the relation.

The main comparison should not allow the learned models to receive an equivalent hand-coded oracle.

## 5.1 Establish an input-access hierarchy

Train carefully controlled models under at least four information conditions.

### Condition O — Hand-computed eligibility reference

The original successful \(m_g\)-injection repair.

This is an upper-reference condition for the targeted relationship.

It is not the main new discovery.

### Condition R — Raw relational ingredients

The model receives:

- Agreement counts \(n_g\).
- Current-question group reports.
- Group identities where required.

It does not receive:

- \(m_g\).
- A perfect-group agreement flag already combined across groups.
- The R1/R2/R3 label.
- The exact posterior.
- The correct answer.

The learner must construct the cross-group conjunction.

### Condition X — Ordinary source inputs

The learner receives only the original source-tokenized episode information, without engineered agreement counts.

This is the strongest test of end-to-end relational computation.

It must learn both the relevant count information and the cross-group relationship.

### Condition Z — Frozen internal representations

The learner receives the actual frozen P-count or T1 activation states at the agreed computational boundary.

It must infer the relationship from the model's own representations.

This distinguishes externally available information from information accessible through the pretrained network.

## 5.2 Architecture comparisons

Include an informative, capacity-controlled set of readers:

1. Linear reader.
2. Nonlinear MLP.
3. Attention-based pairwise group interaction module.
4. Set-structured or permutation-equivariant relational module.
5. Original one-block Transformer downstream architecture.
6. A stronger multi-block Transformer or reader where compute permits.
7. Original hand-computed-\(m_g\) reference.

The purpose is not to compare every architecture on the market.

Choose readers that test different computational requirements.

For example:

- Can a pointwise MLP realize the relation when all ingredients are concatenated?
- Does attention make cross-group comparison easier?
- Does a permutation-equivariant structure improve generalization?
- Is greater depth needed for the conjunction?
- Can the original downstream architecture learn it with adequate optimization?

Explicitly distinguish width, depth, attention access, and parameter count.

Do not attribute an advantage to attention if the attention model simply has substantially greater capacity.

## 5.3 Training objectives

Use at least two separate learning conditions.

**Ordinary task supervision:** train only from candidate-decision labels using the original generator.

**Bayesian teacher supervision:** train the candidate posterior using exact Bayesian soft targets.

The second establishes whether a computation is learnable with stronger supervision.

It is not evidence that ordinary task learning naturally discovers that computation.

Optionally test auxiliary \(m_g\) supervision as a distinct intervention.

But do not mix an explicitly supervised conjunction with a claimed spontaneous discovery.

## 5.4 Main success requirement

A learned model should approach the hand-computed-\(m_g\) repair on:

- R1 response direction and distance to exact Bayes.
- R2/R3 preservation.
- F34/F23 behavior.
- Overall KL and accuracy.
- F0 control magnitudes.
- Unseen source configurations.

Evaluate independent trained models, not just individual episodes.

A model that corrects R1 by simply crediting every perfect group has failed the scientific test.

# 7. FOURTH TASK: IDENTIFY THE INTERNAL OPERATION WHEN LEARNING SUCCEEDS

A successful learned adapter is not enough.

The investigation must ask what was learned.

A model might implement \(m_g\) by:

- Computing perfect-count indicators.
- Comparing group reports.
- Gating source evidence.
- Aggregating compatible groups.
- Approximating the entire posterior directly.
- Learning a different, functionally equivalent representation.

These possibilities must be distinguished where the evidence allows.

## 7.1 Analyze the actual computation

For trained successful models, inspect:

- Token-level representations.
- Source-group and question-specific binding.
- Attention queries, keys, values, and output projections.
- Nonlinear MLP contributions.
- Residual pathways.
- Layer normalization.
- Candidate-specific readout.

Identify which operations respond to the critical cross-group relationship.

Do not infer a mechanism from attention maps alone.

## 7.2 Build a computational account

Try to reconstruct a sequence such as:

\[
\text{source reports}
\rightarrow
\text{agreement information}
\rightarrow
\text{cross-group comparison}
\rightarrow
\text{candidate-specific weighting}
\rightarrow
\text{decision}.
\]

This is a hypothesis to test, not an assumed pipeline.

The actual model may combine operations or use a different factorization.

For each claimed stage, specify:

- What information it contains.
- What transformation is performed.
- How it affects downstream candidate evidence.
- Whether a counterfactual intervention changes the predicted output.
- Which competing explanation the evidence rejects.

## 7.3 Causal interchange and mediation

Use controlled interventions on candidate internal variables.

Test:

- Patching the suspected cross-group relation.
- Altering the relevant equality comparison.
- Changing perfect-group eligibility while preserving other information where feasible.
- Restoring downstream evidence after an earlier intervention.
- Path-specific effects.
- Redundant and compensating routes.

Compare natural source edits with activation interventions.

Report whether internal hybrid states are on-manifold or artificially constructed.

A large activation-patch effect does not identify the natural mediator by itself.

## 7.4 The critical causal test

If an internal learned relational operation has been identified:

**Can a targeted intervention on that operation change the R1 update in the predicted direction while preserving the correct R2/R3 behavior?**

Use matched interventions that affect unrelated information to quantify collateral damage.

Use model-level replication.

Separate:

- Information encoded.
- Information causally used.
- A specific operation necessary for the effect.
- An operation sufficient to reproduce the effect.
- A uniquely identified mechanism.

Do not conflate these claims.

# 9. SIXTH TASK: DISTINGUISH LEARNED COMPOSITION FROM SHORTCUT MEMORIZATION

Because the synthetic generator has a finite state space, high performance does not prove the model learned a reusable compositional rule.

A network could memorize a small table of source configurations.

The investigation must distinguish these possibilities.

## 9.1 Construct systematic holdouts

Develop support-valid train/test partitions that withhold selected relational configurations.

Possible dimensions include:

- Which group is perfect.
- Which group becomes perfect.
- Which candidate the perfect groups support.
- Which pairs of groups agree.
- The identities or ordering of source positions.
- The number and arrangement of perfect groups.
- The relationship between agreement count and current vote.

Preserve the generator's valid configurations.

Avoid hidden leakage through group identity, token position, or repeated episode templates.

## 9.2 Test compositional generalization

Can a model trained on certain source-group relationships correctly handle the same relation under new group assignments?

For example, can it transfer a learned comparison between groups A and B to an equivalent configuration between B and C?

Use exact Bayesian reference calculations.

Compare:

- Hand-computed-\(m_g\) models.
- Raw-ingredient learned adapters.
- Ordinary-token Transformers.
- Simple lookup-table baselines.
- Explicit relational architectures.

Generalization must be demonstrated on held-out configurations, not inferred from high training accuracy.

## 9.3 Intervention invariance

Test whether predictions respect theoretically justified symmetries:

- Permutations of equivalent source groups.
- Candidate relabeling.
- Source-slot permutations where the generator permits them.
- Alternative encodings of the same relational configuration.

A learned eligibility computation should satisfy the appropriate invariance or equivariance conditions.

Do not impose symmetries that the actual generator does not possess.

## 9.4 Carefully controlled generator variation

If the core learning result is established, test a limited set of mathematically related generator variations.

For example:

- Different reliability strengths.
- Different prior probabilities of collusion.
- Small changes in the number of questions.
- Different but compatible source-group arrangements.

Derive the exact Bayesian reference for each.

Distinguish:

- Generalization of the abstract relational operation.
- Generalization of a learned numerical parameter.
- Adaptation after retraining.
- Zero-shot transfer.

Do not claim the same \(m_g\) formula remains exactly sufficient after changing the generator unless it has been proved.

This is a secondary extension, not an excuse to reopen the full COR atlas.

# 10. SEVENTH TASK: BUILD A THEORY OF COMPUTATIONAL ACCESSIBILITY

The earlier work suggests a subtle distinction:

The frozen P-count representations may contain the necessary information, yet the original downstream architecture has difficulty constructing the relevant conjunction.

Develop this distinction mathematically.

## 10.1 Separate four properties

**Information availability**

Does the representation contain enough information to determine the correct relational quantity?

**Decodability**

Can a specified decoder recover that information?

**Computational accessibility**

Can the actual downstream architecture, within its allowed depth, width, and operations, use the information?

**Learnability**

Can the actual optimization process learn the needed computation from the provided data and supervision?

These are not equivalent.

## 10.2 Restricted theorem targets

Seek examples or results where:

- The full information is recoverable by a nonlinear decoder but not by a linear decoder.
- A single restricted computational block cannot implement a specific interaction despite full information availability.
- A wider or differently structured block can implement it.
- The operation is representable but difficult to learn from sparse ordinary supervision.
- Auxiliary relational supervision changes learnability without changing the information available at inference time.
- Two different internal factorizations yield the same candidate-response operator.

State meaningful architectural restrictions.

Do not claim impossibility for universal approximators from finite failed training runs.

## 10.3 Study conditional computational complexity

The problem may not be learning a difficult mathematical function in the abstract.

It may be learning a small function from a representation that makes the required relation geometrically or structurally inconvenient.

Investigate:

- Conjunction depth.
- Interaction order.
- Required feature binding.
- Attention-mediated matching.
- Hidden-state separability.
- Gradient accessibility.
- Function approximation under finite width and data.

If possible, derive restricted lower bounds or constructive upper bounds.

If not, provide honest empirical complexity comparisons.

## 11.1 Abstract computational interface

Define an abstract relational variable or operation independently of any particular Transformer activation basis.

Specify:

- Inputs.
- Output.
- Relevant conditional dependencies.
- Supported interventions.
- Required candidate-response consequences.

## 11.2 Cross-model testing

Determine whether independently trained successful models instantiate an equivalent causal response operator, even if their internal representations differ.

Compare natural counterfactual responses and controlled abstract-variable interventions.

Do not assume raw activation vectors are aligned.

Do not mistake decoder reconstruction accuracy for computational equivalence.

## 11.3 Stronger causal equivalence

Where possible, test whether:

1. Models use the same relational information.
2. That information has corresponding causal effects.
3. The downstream candidate-response maps agree on the supported intervention family.
4. The internal implementations differ.
5. The equivalence holds beyond the training configurations.

A positive result could establish a restricted implementation-independent computational abstraction.

A negative result could establish that different learned operations perform the same task without satisfying the stronger equivalence.

Either is informative.

# 12. NINTH TASK: SELECTIVE CORRECTION WITHOUT HAND-COMPUTED RELATIONAL ORACLES

- A small attention block.
- A learned low-rank adapter.
- Targeted changes to an identified attention or MLP route.

Change supervision, data frequency, curriculum, or optimization without adding inference-time relational features.

# 19. SCIENTIFIC OUTCOMES

Do not force the result into a single predetermined narrative.

There are several meaningful outcomes.

## Outcome A — Relational computation emerges from ordinary task learning

A model trained from ordinary source inputs and task labels learns a computation that reproduces the required relational decision behavior.

It generalizes to unseen relational configurations.

The result does not rely on supplying \(m_g\) or an equivalent feature.

## Outcome B — The relation is learnable only with stronger supervision or architecture

The original setup fails, but a stronger reader, an appropriate auxiliary objective, greater depth, or selective unfreezing enables the correct computation.

This would establish a restricted learning or computational-accessibility limitation.

Do not overstate it as an information-theoretic impossibility.

## Outcome C — The operation is learned and causally identified

A mathematical relational operation is matched to a native internal computation.

Targeted interventions produce the predicted candidate-response effects and preserve unrelated decisions.

This would be a major mechanistic advance.

## Outcome D — Learned selective repair succeeds

A model without hand-computed eligibility at inference substantially corrects the original R1 failure on new independent parents, with R2/R3 preserved.

The repair outperforms appropriately matched generic baselines, or its improvement can be explained through a validated computational account.

## Outcome E — Behavior improves without identifiable mechanism

The model learns a successful mapping, but the internal operation remains ambiguous.

Report functional success without claiming neural causal identification.

## Outcome F — The model learns a shortcut, not a reusable relation

Training performance improves, but the learned mapping fails structured holdouts, permutations, or generator variations.

This is a useful negative result about compositional generalization.

## Outcome G — The representational or optimization bottleneck remains

The relevant information is present, but the tested learners cannot reliably construct and use the relation under the tested conditions.

Characterize the limits of that conclusion carefully.

## Outcome H — Multiple causal implementations exist

Different models solve the relational problem through distinct internal computations.

Determine whether they nevertheless instantiate a restricted common causal response operator.

This could illuminate the original COR question about implementation-independent relational computation.

# 21. FINAL DIRECTIVE

The previous investigations established that agreement-count information can be represented, transferred, and causally useful.

They also showed that the same relational information can support very different candidate decisions.

Investigation VI identified a prospectively reproducible, sign-level decision error at a mathematically meaningful perfect-agreement boundary.

Investigation VII then derived the cross-group eligibility structure required for the exact Bayesian decision and showed that supplying this relationship substantially repairs the error.

But supplying a correct relational function is not the same as discovering how a Transformer learns and computes it.

**Investigation VIII must cross that gap.**

Determine whether the relation can emerge from ordinary evidence and training.

Identify the conditions under which it becomes computationally accessible.

Investigate the learning dynamics that make it appear or prevent it from appearing.

Determine what internal operations successful models actually implement.

Test those operations causally.

Attempt a selective repair that does not require an externally hand-computed eligibility feature at inference.

Evaluate whether the computation generalizes beyond the exact cases used to discover it.

And develop mathematical results explaining the distinction between possessing relational information and successfully using it in a candidate-specific decision.

Do not simply connect existing observations into a plausible story.

**Generate genuinely new, discriminating evidence.**

The target is not merely an improved synthetic-task score.

The target is a rigorous understanding of how a trained Transformer acquires a relational computation, realizes it internally, uses it causally, and succeeds or fails when that computation must influence a decision.

Preserve uncertainty.

Preserve negative results.

Keep the question focused but investigate it deeply.

Remain within Phase 1.5, Step 1.
