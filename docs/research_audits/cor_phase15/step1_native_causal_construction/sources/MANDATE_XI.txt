## 2. THE SCIENTIFIC STARTING POINT

Investigations VIII–X produced a cumulative sequence of results.

**Investigation VIII:** Specialized neural readers learned cross-group relational computations, but the original P-count representation was largely bypassed by the successful adapter.

**Investigation IX:** Full fine-tuning enabled the original Transformer to improve its relational decisions natively across independently pretrained parents. However, the development model's specific causal pathway did not replicate.

**Investigation X:** Multiple repaired Transformers exhibited Bayes-like relational decision behavior and a distributed eligibility-related representation that could be manipulated through learned interchange subspaces.

The strongest Investigation X evidence includes:

- F1: all three complete confirmation parents were closest to the Bayesian eligibility rule among the tested candidate algorithms.
- F2: two of three complete parents satisfied the full frozen abstraction criteria; all three achieved interchange accuracy of at least 0.85.
- F3: the tested per-group eligibility interchange performed better than the pooled-\(S\) intervention.
- F5: the relevant interchange effects were distributed across source and readout tokens.
- F6: eligibility-related subspaces responded selectively to eligibility-defining changes.
- F7: the tested learning trajectories progressed from simpler perfectness-based responses toward Bayes-like responses.
- F4 was falsified: interchange accuracy alone did not reliably distinguish repaired and original models.

These are substantive findings, but they do not yet prove that all models implement identical internal algorithms.

Investigation X also left several important gaps:

1. The mathematical proof supporting part of Theorem X1 contains an invalid argument.
2. The actual computation producing the eligibility-related representation inside fine-tuned layers 0–1 has not been causally reconstructed.
3. Each model's successful interchange alignment was learned separately; direct mapped interchange between models was not tested.
4. Interchange accuracy can be misleading, as demonstrated by F4.
5. No new generalization experiments were completed.
6. The original COR obstruction/discrepancy framework remains unvalidated by these results.

These unresolved issues are the starting point of Investigation XI.

Preserve the successful results, the falsified predictions, and the qualifications.

Do not rewrite historical evidence to make the emerging theory appear more consistent than it is.

## 3. CENTRAL SCIENTIFIC QUESTION

**How do Transformers naturally construct and causally use relational computations, and under what mathematical and experimental conditions can those computations be identified as equivalent across different neural implementations?**

Investigation X established evidence for a shared abstract relational representation at a particular layer boundary.

Investigation XI must investigate what produces that representation, how it enters candidate decisions, whether it genuinely transfers between models, and what mathematical claims can be supported.

Consider competing explanations:

**H1 — Shared relational operation, different implementations**

Models compute the same abstract relational operation using different heads, subspaces, or token pathways.

**H2 — Partial computational equivalence**

Models share some relational operations but differ in how they combine evidence or implement their final decision rules.

**H3 — Task-specific causal abstraction**

The learned eligibility subspace supports the tested interventions but does not capture a general natural computational mediator.

**H4 — Alternative algorithms**

Models approximate similar Bayesian behavior through distinct internal computations that can be differentiated with suitable tests.

**H5 — Incomplete identification**

The current interventions, alignment methods, and mathematical abstractions do not yet distinguish these possibilities.

Do not assume the result must support H1.

A finding that reveals a limitation of our current causal-abstraction methods is also legitimate foundational progress.

## 4. WORKSTREAM A — REPAIR AND EXTEND THE MATHEMATICS

**This workstream is mandatory and should begin before expensive experiments.**

Investigation X introduced Theorem X1, comparing three exact implementations of the same Bayesian decision rule:

- I1: pooled representation.
- I2: per-group representation.
- I3: pairwise representation.

The intended conclusion was that equivalent input-output computations may support different internal interchange interventions.

However, the proof sketch contains a serious problem.

It asserts that different eligibility configurations can produce the same complete per-group \(m\) vector while possessing different total eligibility counts \(S\).

For the three-group case,

\[
m_g=S-e_g
\]

and therefore

\[
S=\frac{m_0+m_1+m_2}{2}.
\]

Thus, equal complete \(m\) vectors necessarily imply equal \(S\).

**The stated counterexample cannot exist.**

### A1. Independently audit Theorem X1

Re-derive the theorem from the definitions and source code.

Determine:

- Which exact interchange constructions are mathematically valid.
- Which claims of impossibility have rigorous proofs.
- Which conclusions rely only on unsuccessful numerical optimization.
- Whether a different linear subspace can implement a supposedly impossible intervention.
- Whether the statement depends on restricting the intervention map, representation space, or decoder.
- Whether the normalized posterior changes the apparent identifiability result.

Construct explicit counterexamples where appropriate.

Do not automatically preserve the theorem.

Possible outcomes include a corrected theorem, a weaker restricted proposition, a counterexample, or a retraction of part of the result.

### A2. Check the interventions themselves

The high-level operation "set \(S\) while holding \(e_g\) fixed" can violate the defining relationship

\[
S=\sum_g e_g.
\]

Distinguish mathematically legitimate internal interventions from naturally realizable input configurations.

Investigate whether apparent interchange failures result from:

- Inconsistent high-level variable assignments.
- Off-support or unnatural interventions.
- Restricted linear maps.
- Nonlinear representations.
- Optimization failure.
- Genuine differences in computational factorization.

A theorem about one artificial intervention family should not be presented as a universal theorem about neural computation.

### A3. Seek useful mathematical consequences

Once the foundational claims have been checked, investigate whether stronger restricted results can be derived concerning:

- Computational equivalence versus implementation equivalence.
- Information sufficiency.
- Causal abstraction and identifiability.
- Distributed versus localized relational representations.
- Redundancy and alternative computational factorizations.
- Conditions under which different algorithms are observationally indistinguishable.

Preserve connections to the Bayesian eligibility formula from Investigation IX, with its exact assumptions.

Do not manufacture a theorem merely to produce a mathematical deliverable.

A rigorous counterexample or corrected limitation may be more valuable than an unsupported generalization.

## 5. WORKSTREAM B — RECONSTRUCT THE NATIVE CAUSAL COMPUTATION

**This is the principal mechanistic investigation.**

Investigation X found an eligibility-related representation at the downstream input after fine-tuned layers 0–1.

But the mechanism that constructs this representation remains unidentified.

Investigate how the network combines two ingredients:

1. Whether a group has perfect leave-question agreement.
2. Whether its sources agree at the target question.

These quantities must be combined appropriately for the exact relational eligibility rule.

### B1. Localize the actual computation

Use existing checkpoints to examine:

- Attention-head contributions.
- Q/K/V/O pathways.
- MLP operations.
- Residual-stream interactions.
- Relevant source and readout tokens.
- Information transmission between groups.
- Candidate-specific downstream effects.

Prioritize head-level and pathway-level causal interventions within layers 0–1.

Investigation X's early-MLP total-effect measurements are not enough: an early component can carry the entire downstream response without itself computing the relational operation.

Distinguish information propagation from relational combination.

### B2. Establish a causal chain

Where the evidence permits, reconstruct a chain of the form:

**Input relationships → internal relational computation → downstream relational representation → candidate-specific evidence update.**

Use interventions that selectively alter:

- Perfectness information.
- Agreement at the target question.
- Their conjunction.
- Candidate evidence.
- Unrelated control features.

Ask whether a proposed component is necessary, sufficient under stated conditions, or one of several redundant contributors.

Compare intervention effects on R1, R2, R3, and mathematically discriminating configurations.

Use restoration experiments and matched controls.

Do not describe a component as the natural causal mediator merely because its activation is decodable or can be patched successfully.

### B3. Compare independent implementations

Investigate whether different pretrained parents use:

- The same abstract operation in different heads.
- Distinct combinations of heads and MLPs.
- Distributed redundant pathways.
- Different operations that produce similar downstream representations.

Use head-permutation-aware comparisons where appropriate.

Preserve the distinction between parent-specific mechanism identity and genuine cross-parent computational invariance.

### B4. Explore learning dynamics where informative

Investigation X reported that models learn a simpler perfectness-only rule before integrating target-question agreement.

Use available checkpoints to test whether the emergence of the actual causal mechanism matches this behavioral transition.

Does the required information become available first, followed by relational integration?

Or does the computation arise through a different process?

Do not launch another extensive training study unless existing checkpoints are insufficient to distinguish the main hypotheses.

## 6. WORKSTREAM C — TEST CROSS-MODEL ABSTRACTION AND IDENTIFICATION VALIDITY

Investigation X found strong within-model interchange results, but a learned alignment was fitted separately for each parent.

**Direct cross-model interchange remains unresolved.**

### C1. Mapped cross-model interchange

Investigate whether a representation learned in one fine-tuned model can be transferred into another model through an independently fitted alignment.

Compare:

- Within-model interchange.
- Cross-model mapped interchange.
- Matched random or unrelated subspaces.
- Models sharing the same pretrained parent but different fine-tuning seeds.
- Models with independently pretrained parents.

Fit mappings without using confirmation outcomes.

Evaluate both transfer directions where feasible.

Test whether a mapping preserves the candidate-response consequences of eligibility changes, rather than simply matching activation statistics.

### C2. Examine the F4 failure

Original C1 models sometimes achieved surprisingly high interchange accuracy despite failing the correct relational decision rule.

Investigation X suggested that selectivity provides a necessary additional diagnostic.

Test this more rigorously.

Compare:

- Interchange accuracy.
- Eligibility-defining edit selectivity.
- Matched control-edit sensitivity.
- Counterfactual candidate-response fidelity.
- Preservation of unrelated decision information.
- Dependence on alignment-training objectives.

Determine whether the combination identifies a natural relational mediator more reliably than interchange accuracy alone.

Do not assume that selectivity plus interchange is universally sufficient.

Seek false positives and false negatives.

### C3. Distinguish variable identification from causal explanation

Ask whether the learned subspace:

- Represents a relational variable.
- Causally mediates the native decision.
- Encodes multiple entangled variables.
- Allows an external intervention to emulate the decision without identifying the model's natural computation.

This distinction is central to defending COR's causal arguments.

If the evidence only establishes a restricted interchange abstraction, report exactly that.

## 14. FINAL DIRECTIVE

The COR program is investigating how relationships among information sources become computationally meaningful and causally affect AI decisions.

Investigations VIII–X established progressively stronger evidence that neural systems can learn relational evidence-update computations, improve their decisions natively, and exhibit similar high-level representations despite differences in internal pathways.

But several foundational questions remain unresolved.

A successful interchange is not automatically evidence of a uniquely identified natural algorithm.

Similar decisions do not automatically imply identical computations.

A mathematical representation does not automatically reveal what a Transformer computes.

And a numerical failure to find an alignment is not proof that no alignment exists.

**Investigation XI must strengthen the connection between mathematical structure, naturally learned internal operations, causal intervention evidence, and candidate-specific decisions.**

Correct the mathematical foundations where necessary.

Identify the computation rather than merely its downstream representation.

Test whether causal abstractions transfer across neural implementations.
