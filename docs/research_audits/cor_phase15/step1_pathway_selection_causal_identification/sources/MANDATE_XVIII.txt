# 1. THE DISCOVERY WE ARE INVESTIGATING

Investigation XIV established the following in a controlled T4 cohort:

A temporary 100-step block of a selected information pathway during training changed the eventual organization of the learned solution.

The effect occurred in six of six tested base-by-data-order runs.

An off-route masking control changed the organization in zero of six runs.

A disturbance applied to the same attention edges, matched by attention-total-variation severity, also changed the organization in zero of six runs.

The intervention's effect persisted after the block was removed.

However, the cohort used one pretrained parent, two fine-tuned organizational bases, and three data orders per base.

The six runs were not six independent pretrained models.

**The original result is real within its tested conditions, but its exact cause and generality remain unresolved.**

## A critical limitation in XIV

The real block reduced or eliminated access through the targeted pathway.

But both controls increased the attention share allocated to the original pathway.

Therefore, the controls did not establish whether the learning change resulted from:

1. Specifically removing the information available through the pathway.
2. Reducing that pathway's relative contribution to the neural computation.
3. Redirecting optimization toward an alternative route.
4. A broader training disturbance associated with the intervention.

The distinction is essential.

The next experiment must confront it directly.

# 2. MANDATORY DESIGN REVIEW BEFORE ANY NEW TRAINING

**This is the most important procedural change after XVII.**

XVII spent substantial compute confirming outcomes that were mathematically guaranteed or incapable of distinguishing the competing explanations.

That must not happen again.

Before a single new confirmation model is trained, perform three checks.

## Check A — Is the answer already determined?

Determine whether a proposed result follows directly from:

- The architecture.
- The attention softmax equations.
- The intervention definition.
- The task generator.
- The mathematical scoring rule.
- A guaranteed property of the initialized models.

If so, derive it mathematically.

Do not spend hours confirming it experimentally.

## Check B — Can the experiment distinguish the explanations?

For every proposed intervention, construct a prediction table.

Show what each competing explanation predicts.

If two explanations produce the same result under the proposed manipulation, that result cannot distinguish them.

Reject or redesign non-discriminating tests.

## Check C — Is the finding already known?

Conduct a focused literature search before finalizing the experimental design.

Compare with research on:

- Circuit formation during neural training.
- Attention masking and information routing.
- Competition between learned neural algorithms.
- Path dependence and multiple training solutions.
- Training-time causal interventions.
- Curriculum effects and early learning constraints.
- Mechanistic interpretability of algorithm selection.

Identify what is already established.

Do not call the broad idea of pathway competition novel merely because the specific T4 generator is new.

## Independent pre-experiment review

At least two independent reviewing agents must challenge the experimental design before the freeze.

One should examine the mathematics and causal identifiability.

The other should examine alternative neural and optimization explanations.

Their approval must depend on the experiment actually distinguishing competing hypotheses, not merely being reproducible.

If the primary test is non-identifying, redesign it.

**Do not proceed to expensive confirmation merely to satisfy the mandate's requested number of runs.**

Produce a written design-review decision.

# 3. FORMALIZE THE COMPETING CAUSAL EXPLANATIONS

Develop the strongest mathematically meaningful versions of these explanations.

## H1 — Information-access dependence

The learned organization changes because the model cannot obtain particular relational information through the targeted pathway during an important period of learning.

This explanation predicts that interventions affecting the actual availability of the relevant information should influence which computation is learned.

But the explanation requires a precise definition of information availability.

A pathway carrying a tiny attention weight is not automatically equivalent to a pathway carrying zero information.

Define the distinction mathematically.

## H2 — Relative contribution and pathway competition

The change occurs because the targeted pathway contributes less to the network's computation, allowing an alternative pathway to become more influential during optimization.

Under this explanation, sufficiently reducing the effective contribution of a route may produce similar outcomes even when its attention edges remain technically accessible.

The quantity of interest may be attention mass, transmitted signal, gradient contribution, or some combination.

Do not assume those are equivalent.

## H3 — General optimization disturbance

The intervention changes the training process through its effects on loss, gradients, or optimization stability, rather than through a particular relational information route.

Other interventions producing comparable training disturbances might cause similar organizational changes.

## H4 — Interaction-dependent selection

The effect depends on both the specific information pathway and its quantitative contribution to training.

There may be no sharp distinction between removing access and reducing its influence.

The mathematical equations may make some formulations of H1 and H2 observationally equivalent.

If so, demonstrate the equivalence instead of forcing an artificial distinction.

## H5 — Parent- or initialization-specific behavior

The intervention's ability to change the learned organization may depend on a particular pretrained representation or fine-tuning initialization.

Under this explanation, the original result may not generalize to genuinely independent pretrained models.

### Mandatory requirement

Each hypothesis must produce at least one discriminating prediction or be explicitly marked as non-identifiable.

Do not allow every hypothesis to survive every possible outcome.

# 4. EXPERIMENT A — DIRECTLY TEST THE MISSING CONTROL

**Highest-priority neural experiment.**

The key missing control is an intervention that decreases the targeted pathway's contribution without simply reproducing the original complete block.

## A1. Recover the exact pathway definitions

Reconstruct the real interventions from XIII-E and XIV.

The original study used different route locations in its early and mixture organizations.

Verify the precise tensor indices, attention edges, layer identities, and source sets from the implementation.

Check that the published descriptions of real-route and off-route edges correspond to the actual code.

Do not rely on verbal descriptions if the tensor masks disagree with them.

## A2. Continuous pathway suppression

Construct a graded intervention that decreases attention to the relevant edges without setting their logits to negative infinity.

For example, use controlled finite negative attention-logit offsets.

Develop a range of strengths from minimal suppression to near-complete suppression.

Measure:

- Attention mass on the targeted edges.
- Effective information contribution.
- Changes in forward activations.
- Training loss.
- Gradient norms and directions.
- Alternative-route activity.
- Final learned organization.
- Model competence.

The central question is whether a change in learned organization requires a complete block or can emerge under partial suppression.

## A3. Alternative means of reducing pathway contribution

Construct a second intervention that reduces the original route's contribution by a different mechanism.

A possible approach is increasing the attention logits of a carefully controlled competing route rather than directly suppressing the target edges.

Other approaches may be appropriate if mathematically justified.

The second mechanism should be chosen so that the proposed explanations predict different outcomes.

Do not assume that positive bias to an alternative route is harmless.

Verify what information that route carries and how its contribution changes.

## A4. Information-content intervention

Where technically and mathematically meaningful, manipulate the information transmitted through the route while approximately preserving its attention allocation.

This may involve controlled changes to value content or other representations.

Require controls preserving ordinary source evidence and task semantics.

Do not claim that value perturbation cleanly isolates information content unless the intervention and its controls justify that claim.

## A5. Matched comparisons

Compare interventions on:

- Target attention share.
- Effective signal contribution.
- Attention-total-variation distance.
- Immediate loss disruption.
- Gradient disturbance.
- Training duration.
- Total optimization exposure.
- Final competence.

Some of these quantities may be impossible to match simultaneously.

If so, derive and report the tradeoffs.

Do not use the term "matched severity" without specifying the metric.

Use development data to calibrate the intervention strength.

Freeze the calibration procedure before confirmation.

Do not tune severity on confirmation outcomes.

## A6. The decisive comparison

Compare:

**A — Full targeted block.**

**B — Partial targeted suppression with the route still accessible.**

**C — Reduced route contribution achieved through an alternative intervention.**

**D — Matched non-targeted disturbance.**

**E — A source- or signal-content control, if identifiable.**

The outcome must be evaluated by the final learned computation, not just its accuracy.

A result where multiple distinct interventions change the organization at the same effective route contribution would support a different explanation from one where only eliminating a particular informational pathway works.

Define those distinctions prospectively.

# 5. EXPERIMENT B — ESTABLISH THE ACTUAL LEARNED ORGANIZATION

**Mandatory.**

One lesson from XIII–XVII is that a useful-looking measurement may not uniquely identify the mechanism it claims to measure.

The original pathway experiment used organizational scores involving attention behavior.

Some of those scores directly involved the edges that were manipulated.

Therefore, the new study must not rely on a single attention-derived score to conclude that a different algorithm was learned.

## B1. Independent behavioral diagnosis

Test the trained models on mathematically defined relational cases that distinguish the known T4 organizations.

Use:

- Exact Bayesian references.
- The previously validated early-versus-mixture behavioral distinctions.
- Relevant counterfactual source edits.
- Relational evidence changes.
- Previously validated role or reliability contrasts.

Determine whether the models genuinely differ in how they combine evidence.

## B2. Independent neural diagnosis

Use validated causal tests from XIII–XIV where appropriate.

Measure:

- Which pathways are necessary.
- Whether alternative pathways are sufficient.
- Whether the relevant relational information is causally used.
- Whether the organization remains after the intervention is removed.
- Whether attention changes correspond to different causal dependencies.

Avoid unsupported claims about a unique three-dimensional carrier or an identifiable fitted combination operator.

Those earlier claims were qualified or withdrawn.

## B3. Competence

Every organizational conclusion must be accompanied by a test of task competence.

A model that no longer solves the task is not evidence that a competent alternative strategy was selected.

Distinguish:

- Competent organizational change.
- Hybrid organization.
- Temporary disruption.
- Failed learning.
- Measurement ambiguity.

## B4. Classification rule

Freeze the organizational classification before new confirmation outcomes are inspected.

Require multiple independently informative diagnostics.

When diagnostics disagree, report an ambiguous or hybrid result.

Do not force a binary classification.

# 6. EXPERIMENT C — GENERALIZATION ACROSS INDEPENDENT PARENTS

**Mandatory if the causal comparison in Experiment A is diagnostic and computationally feasible.**

The XIV effect was established primarily within one pretrained parent.

Changing the data order does not create a new independent pretrained model.

The next question is whether the causal effect survives genuine changes in pretraining initialization and learned representations.

## C1. Independent pretrained parents

Use models derived from genuinely independent pretraining initializations and data streams.

Prefer at least three independent pretrained parents in the total confirmation cohort, including the historically studied parent only as a reference.

Do not count fine-tuning rerolls from one pretrained parent as independent pretraining replications.

If the budget cannot support that design, clearly distinguish established within-parent evidence from incomplete across-parent evidence.

## C2. Establish the feasible organizations

The original COR record shows that different pretrained parents do not necessarily produce both early and mixture organizations under the same recipe.

Do not manufacture nominal comparisons that lack competent baseline models.

For each parent:

- Determine whether competent organizational alternatives are obtainable.
- Record failed attempts.
- Use predefined selection rules.
- Preserve the complete denominator of candidate parents.
- Avoid choosing only parents that display the desired result.

## C3. Paired intervention comparison

Within each valid parent, compare:

- Baseline training.
- Real route block.
- Partial route suppression.
- Matched alternative disturbance.
- Any additional decisive intervention justified in Experiment A.

Use matched initializations and data order where appropriate.

Then compare effects across independent parents.

## C4. Scientific outcome

Determine whether:

- The effect is specific to the historical parent.
- It generalizes to multiple independent parents.
- Its direction depends on pre-existing organization.
- Its magnitude depends on identifiable representations.
- Particular parents provide counterexamples.

A failed generalization is a scientific result.

Do not hide it.

# 7. EXPERIMENT D — WHEN DOES THE INTERVENTION MATTER?

**Conditional secondary experiment.**

XIV suggested that a short intervention during formation has a lasting effect, while an equivalent intervention after organization forms is often reversed.

But earlier studies also showed that sufficiently long late interventions can cause lasting changes.

Therefore, there is no established closed critical period.

If the main causal experiment succeeds, investigate whether intervention timing changes the final organization under matched conditions.

Control:

- Intervention duration.
- Learning-rate exposure.
- Effective suppression.
- Recovery opportunity.
- Model competence.
- Initial organization.
- Number of post-release updates.

The question is whether early training creates a special sensitivity to pathway competition, not whether the network becomes permanently immutable.

Do not repeat the previous persistence measurements without a new discriminating hypothesis.

# 16. FINAL SCIENTIFIC DIRECTIVE

The COR research program has spent substantial time investigating relational learning, implementation heterogeneity, neural causal mechanisms, and training dynamics.

Many findings were useful.

Several strong interpretations were withdrawn after better controls.

Investigation XVII demonstrated the danger of performing expensive experiments that cannot distinguish the competing explanations.

Investigation XVIII must correct that research pattern.

It must focus on a result that already has credible causal evidence and important unresolved questions.

**The aim is to determine whether temporary restriction of a relational information pathway selects a learned computation because of information deprivation, attention competition, broader optimization effects, or an identifiable combination of these mechanisms.**

The work must not become another repetition of earlier route-blocking experiments.

It must not expand into an uncontrolled search across architectures and generators.

It must not claim an original COR prediction where none exists.

It must not call a learned organization a distinct algorithm without the relevant independent functional and causal evidence.

It must not infer generality from different fine-tuning seeds of the same pretrained parent.

It must not force a novel conclusion.

**The minimum successful outcome is a controlled experiment that genuinely distinguishes explanations the previous investigations could not distinguish.**

The stronger outcome is a result that remains valid across independent pretrained models and supports a precise scientific claim not already established by existing research.

A falsification, boundary, or carefully demonstrated non-identifiability is also a meaningful result.

The result should tell us something that was genuinely unknown before the investigation began.

Remain in COR Phase 1.5, Step 1, with approximately 25% provisional foundational exploration.

No phase closure, automatic exploration increase, or validation of the original O/D framework.

**Execute Investigation XVIII only after the pre-experiment mathematical and causal design has passed independent adversarial review. Deliver the complete verified evidence, not merely a list of successful verdicts.**
