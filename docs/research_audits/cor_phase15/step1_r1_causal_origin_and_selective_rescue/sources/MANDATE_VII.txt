# 1. THE ESTABLISHED RESULT YOU MUST EXPLAIN

Investigation VI established a prospective result using 16 independently seeded parent models, eight T1-pretrained and eight P-count-pretrained.

Both curricula were transferred to T4 with:

- Embeddings and layers 0–1 frozen.
- Layer 2 freshly initialized and trained.
- The inherited answer readout and final normalization parameters participating in transfer.
- Shared target-task training and evaluation procedures.

The task has:

- Three candidate classes.
- Six questions.
- Three groups of two sources.
- The specified T4 latent-reliability/collusion generator.
- Perfect-collusion parameter ρ = 1.

The important configuration is R1.

A source group changes from 4/5 agreement to 5/5 agreement on the other questions, while the newly perfect group's vote agrees with the candidate supported by an already-perfect group.

The exact Bayesian decision rule says this transition should increase support for that candidate.

|---|---:|
| Exact Bayesian reference | +0.234 |
| T1-pretrained Transformers | +0.193 |

\[
0.698

# 3. THE THREE PRINCIPAL CAUSAL EXPLANATIONS

The new research must discriminate the following explanations.

## H-REP — Frozen representation limitation

P-count pretraining supplies agreement-count information, but its frozen layers 0–1 do not make the necessary *conditional cross-group evidence* sufficiently accessible to the third layer.

The model can represent the count but cannot readily distinguish the decision significance of a newly perfect group that agrees with another perfect group.

Possible subcases include:

- Incomplete count information.
- Information distributed across source and readout tokens.
- Inadequate binding of group identity, source vote, and count.
- A saturated perfect-agreement representation.
- Entanglement of count information with irrelevant source details.
- A representation requiring a computational operation that layer 2 cannot easily learn within the transfer budget.

A failed linear probe is not proof of information absence.

## H-READ — Inherited readout or normalization bias

The frozen early representations may contain sufficient information, but the inherited answer-readout matrix or final LayerNorm affects the downstream solution.

This possibility is especially relevant because:

- T1 pretraining trains the answer readout directly.
- P-count pretraining uses a different auxiliary head.
- The P-count answer readout did not receive the same task gradient during pretraining.
- Both the inherited answer readout and final normalization participate in T4 transfer.

The earlier curriculum comparison therefore does not isolate the effect of the pretraining objective.

## H-LEARN — Incorrect transfer-learned evidence-update rule

The necessary conditional information is available, but the newly trained third layer learns an overly general response to perfect agreement.

For example, it might treat reaching 5/5 as evidence for a colluder configuration and discount the associated candidate without adequately conditioning on how the group's vote relates to other perfect groups.

This is a possible explanation, not an established mechanism.

The learned rule may involve attention, value transformations, MLP interactions, and residual computation jointly.

### Additional hypothesis: H-INT — Interaction among the causes

The actual explanation may require both a particular frozen representation and a downstream learner or readout bias.

Do not force all observations into a single-locus explanation.

A causal explanation should establish the relevant interaction if one exists.

### Required task

For each hypothesis, derive predictions that differ from its strongest competitors.

Before running new confirmation experiments, specify what observation would support, weaken, or falsify the hypothesis.

Do not select the favored explanation after the result.

# 4. FIRST MATHEMATICAL TASK: CHARACTERIZE WHAT THE CORRECT DECISION REQUIRES

The experiments should begin from the exact Bayesian decision, not from an assumed neural circuit.

For an episode x and candidate pair (c,c′), define the model margin as:

\[
m_M(x;c,c')
=
\log p_M(c\mid x)-\log p_M(c'\mid x).
\]

For a supported edit \(x\rightarrow x'\), define:

\[
\Delta_M=m_M(x')-m_M(x),
\]

and similarly \(\Delta_B\) for the exact Bayesian reference.

The model's Bayes-corrected response is:

\[
E_M=\Delta_M-\Delta_B.
\]

This is the primary behavioral object.

## 4.1 Derive the R1 inference requirement

For the exact T4 generator, determine which information is needed to distinguish:

- R1: the newly perfect group's vote agrees with the existing perfect group's candidate.
- R2: the corresponding neutral or differently informative configuration.
- R3: the configuration where the newly perfect group supports a conflicting candidate.
- Other supported configurations where perfect agreement has different decision implications.

Derive the relevant likelihood or posterior expressions.

Specify whether successful inference needs:

1. The group's agreement count.
2. Its vote on the target question.
3. The other groups' counts.
4. The other groups' candidate votes.
5. The posterior over source-role configurations.
6. A smaller candidate-conditioned statistic.

Determine the smallest useful sufficient or partially sufficient representation under explicit assumptions.

Do not infer that the complete count vector V alone is sufficient for the whole candidate decision; the target question's evidence also matters.

## 4.2 Examine the explaining-away structure

The R1 failure may arise because the model interprets perfect agreement without correctly conditioning on the alternative explanations for that agreement.

Characterize mathematically:

- The relevant latent configurations before the edit.
- The new configurations made possible at 5/5.
- How probability mass changes between reliable and colluding roles.
- Why the R1 Bayesian candidate response is positive.
- Why other configurations may require a negative response.
- Which conditional dependencies the model must implement to distinguish them.

Do not use “explaining away” as a label without specifying the actual latent conditional probabilities.

## 4.3 Develop discriminating computational examples

Construct restricted computational rules such as:

- Count-only reliability weighting.
- Independent groupwise weighting.
- Global latent-configuration inference.
- Perfect-agreement categorical discounting.
- Candidate-conditioned group interaction.
- Exact Bayes.
- Strong approximation baselines.

Determine which rules predict the wrong R1 sign and which preserve the correct signs across R1/R2/R3.

Where possible, derive restricted theorems.

Where not, provide exact enumeration and counterexamples.

**Required mathematical result:** the minimum information and conditional operation required for the correct R1 response, together with explicit examples of approximations that fail.

# 5. SECOND TASK: TEST WHETHER THE FROZEN REPRESENTATIONS ARE SUFFICIENT

This is the highest-leverage discrimination between H-REP and H-LEARN.

A count probe showing 95–100% decodability is not enough.

We need to determine whether the frozen state contains the information required to compute the correct Bayesian decision.

Let:

\[
Z_{\mathrm{pre2}}(x)
\]

be the complete 43-token residual state entering layer 2, after the frozen pretrained layers.

For each curriculum, ask whether a downstream computation receiving \(Z_{\mathrm{pre2}}\) can recover the correct candidate posterior and R1 response.

## 5.1 Train controlled Bayesian readers

Freeze the original embeddings and layers 0–1.

Train several downstream readers using only those frozen model states.

Include:

**Reader A — Original architecture**

Fresh layer 2, final normalization, and answer readout, with carefully controlled initialization.

**Reader B — More flexible Transformer readout**

Sufficient capacity to combine information across source and readout tokens.

**Reader C — Controlled nonlinear reader**

A non-attention architecture with access to the complete frozen state.

**Reader D — Explicit sufficient-statistic reference**

Receives the exact generator-derived candidate evidence and relational statistic.

This is a positive control, not an equally informed neural competitor.

**Reader E — Restricted-site reader**

Receives only the original target readout state or only selected source states.

This helps locate information limitations.

Capacity-match the relevant readers where meaningful, and report unavoidable differences explicitly.

Train on natural, generator-supported episodes.

Use independent evaluation data.

Do not include the exact Bayesian posterior, latent source roles, or an oracle regime label as test-time input unless the reader is explicitly designated as an oracle control.

The reader may be supervised with Bayes probabilities during training for a **sufficiency test**. Such success shows that the frozen representation can support Bayes-like predictions under that supervision and capacity; it does not show that the original model learned or naturally uses that algorithm.

## 5.2 Compare information accessibility

For each reader and curriculum, evaluate:

- Exact Bayesian KL.
- R1 4→5 response.
- R2/R3 responses.
- F34/F23 responses.
- Natural accuracy.
- Rare-event conditional accuracy.
- Performance under different training budgets.
- Sensitivity to source identity and group binding.
- Held-out source configurations.

Determine whether a correct R1 rule can be learned from the frozen P-count states.

If a sufficiently controlled downstream reader consistently solves R1, that weakens claims of fundamental information insufficiency.

If no tested reader succeeds, do not immediately claim the information is absent.

Distinguish:

- Information-theoretic insufficiency.
- Architecture-limited accessibility.
- Optimization failure.
- Limited training signal.
- Data insufficiency.

## 5.3 Seek stronger sufficiency evidence

Investigate whether there exist distinct supported episodes with indistinguishable frozen representations but different required Bayesian decisions.

If exact representation collisions occur, characterize their implications.

If only approximate collisions occur, state the metric and assumptions under which a lower bound on downstream prediction error follows.

Where a rigorous information-theoretic impossibility cannot be established, report empirical evidence rather than an impossibility theorem.

## 5.4 Separate count availability from conditional use

Test whether the frozen representation contains not just the counts, but the relation between:

- Which group became perfect.
- Which candidate it supports.
- Which other group is already perfect.
- Whether the votes agree.

A representation may encode each item separately while failing to place them in a computationally accessible joint form.

Determine whether the model's downstream information access is local, distributed, or global.

**Required result:** an experimentally grounded answer to whether the frozen P-count representation contains enough usable information for a correct R1 decision, with precise limits on what the tests establish.

- A readout-token patch that reproduced the sign difference across 16 models, but only exploratorily.
- Evidence in four models that layer-2 attention and MLP contributions interact non-additively.

The new study must address those limitations.

## 7.1 Start from the full forward equation

For the relevant readout token, reconstruct:

1. The frozen residual input to layer 2.
2. Pre-attention LayerNorm.
3. Attention queries, keys, values, and attention weights.
4. Value and output projections.
5. Attention-output residual addition.
6. Pre-MLP LayerNorm.
7. GELU MLP computation.
8. MLP residual addition.
9. Final LayerNorm.
10. Candidate-logit readout.

Trace the actual R1 counterfactual change through the whole computation.

Do not assume the logit response is an additive sum of independently measured pathway effects.

Use exact forward replays to quantify those interactions.

## 7.2 Test the same computational sites in both curricula

Begin with the same readout-token position `36 + q` at the input to layer 2.

Also examine the six source-token states and relevant non-target tokens.

Do not repeat the earlier comparison of a 64-dimensional P-count site against a 384-dimensional T1 multi-token site and call it a matched localization result.

## 7.3 Separate attention and MLP contributions

For a representative model panel and then independent confirmation models, investigate:

- Individual attention heads.
- Query-side effects.
- Key-side effects.
- Value-side effects.
- Output-projection effects.
- Attention normalization.
- MLP nonlinear response.
- Residual bypass paths.
- Final LayerNorm.

**Which specific operation turns the R1 input change into a negative candidate-margin update in P-count models?**

A correct reconstruction must predict the sign, not merely identify components that affect accuracy.

## 7.4 Quantify non-additivity

The previous exploratory work found that the P-count discount could require attention and MLP changes jointly.

Investigate this interaction systematically.

Where a factorial intervention over pathways is meaningful, compare:

- Neither component changed.
- Attention change only.
- MLP change only.
- Both changed.
- Full natural counterfactual.

Define interaction effects explicitly.

Distinguish computational interactions from artifacts of inconsistent hybrid states.

Do not use arbitrary partial patches as if they were natural intermediate activations.

## 7.5 Test redundancy and cancellation

Earlier COR investigations repeatedly found multiple pathways, redundancy, and compensating effects.

Ablating one component may leave the decision intact because other components carry the information.

Test joint-path interventions.

Determine whether the negative R1 update results from:

- One dominant operation.
- Several cooperating operations.
- Several partially cancelling operations.
- A downstream nonlinear interaction.
- A residual baseline plus correction.
- A distributed conditional computation.

Do not identify a unique mediator merely because a single-path patch has a large effect.

**Required result:** the strongest actual computational explanation of the wrong R1 update, including the mathematical limits of causal attribution.

# 9. SIXTH TASK: DESIGN A SELECTIVE CORRECTION

This is the most important practical and causal test.

The goal is not merely to improve average accuracy.

The goal is to correct the P-count model's wrong R1 evidence update **without destroying the legitimate reliability and collusion computations elsewhere**.

A general rule such as “always credit perfect agreement” is not acceptable.

That rule would be wrong in other source configurations.

## 9.1 Define the rescue target

For a repaired model \(M^*\), require:

1. R1 4→5 candidate updates move toward the correct Bayesian response.
2. The sign reversals responsible for the original failure are substantially reduced.
3. R2 and R3 updates remain correct where they were correct before.
4. Ordinary F34/F23 responses are preserved.
5. Count-preserving responses are not made substantially worse.
6. Overall T4 accuracy and KL do not degrade materially.
7. The effect generalizes to held-out episodes and independent models.
8. The repair uses only information available to the model at inference time.

Do not select favorable evaluation subsets after optimization.

## 9.2 Compare different repair classes

Investigate at least three scientifically distinct approaches.

### Repair class A — Representation rescue

Improve or supplement the frozen relational representation so that the downstream computation can access the needed conditional information.

Examples include:

- An independently specified group-relationship feature.
- A trainable low-rank representation adapter.
- A minimal additional attention route.
- Selective unfreezing of the relevant frozen computation.

Determine whether this rescues R1 under an otherwise fixed downstream learner.

Do not insert the exact answer as a new feature.

### Repair class B — Downstream computation rescue

Keep the frozen representation fixed.

Modify only the downstream evidence-update computation.

Candidates include:

- Reinitializing and retraining selected layer-2 components.
- A low-rank layer-2 update.
- A targeted attention or MLP transformation.
- A constrained conditional evidence operator.
- Replacement of an identified error-producing computational subpath.

The intervention should be justified by the mechanism analysis, not chosen solely because it changes the R1 logits.

### Repair class C — Readout rescue

Keep the frozen features and most of layer 2 fixed.

Investigate changes to:

- The answer readout.
- Final normalization.
- A limited candidate-evidence transformation.

Determine whether the wrong R1 sign originates in how the final representation is interpreted.

## 9.3 Include non-mechanistic repair baselines

Compare with:

- Ordinary extra T4 fine-tuning.
- Additional end-to-end training.
- A capacity-matched small adapter placed at a random site.
- A generic low-rank update.
- A final-logit recalibration.
- Training on additional naturally sampled R1 examples.
- A strong oracle-Bayes reader as an upper-bound control.

These are essential.

A successful mechanistically motivated repair is scientifically more informative if it achieves something that generic fine-tuning or extra data does not achieve as efficiently or selectively.

But if ordinary training fixes the problem, report that result.

Do not force a complicated causal intervention to win.

## 9.4 Do not permit an oracle shortcut

At evaluation time, the repaired model must not receive:

- The exact Bayesian posterior.
- The correct answer.
- A generator-supplied R1/R2/R3 label.
- The latent true source roles.

It must infer the relevant information from its ordinary available inputs or learned internal states.

Explicitly identify what supervision the repair receives during training.

A repair trained directly on Bayesian targets can establish learnability or sufficiency. It does not by itself prove the original model naturally implemented the same computation.

## 9.5 Separate behavioral correction from causal explanation

Suppose a small adapter fixes R1.

That is a behavioral rescue.

It becomes a stronger mechanistic result only if the adapter's effect follows from a previously identified computational deficiency and the intervention has a specific, independently tested causal interpretation.

Do not retroactively claim that whatever edit worked was the original failure mechanism.

**Required result:** a selective rescue attempt with matched alternatives, explicit causal interpretation, and preservation tests.

A failed selective rescue is also informative if it establishes why the proposed mechanism is insufficient.

# 11. EIGHTH TASK: DISTINGUISH CAUSAL MECHANISM FROM APPROXIMATION ERROR

The R1 reversal is a structured approximation error relative to the exact Bayesian reference.

But saying “approximation error” does not explain why the error occurs.

Develop a causal-mathematical model of the possible failure.

For example, suppose the computation contains:

1. A relational feature \(r\) derived from the source evidence.
2. A candidate-conditioned feature \(u\).
3. An evidence transformation \(F_\theta(r,u)\).
4. A final candidate-margin readout.

Investigate whether the P-count models fail because:

- \(r\) discards necessary information.
- \(r\) is adequate but poorly aligned with \(u\).
- \(F_\theta\) lacks the required interaction.
- The learned \(F_\theta\) implements the wrong interaction.
- The correct transformation is present but cancelled downstream.
- The inherited readout maps a useful representation incorrectly.
- Optimization favors a shortcut because the relevant configuration is rare.

This factorization is provisional.

Do not force it onto the model if the actual computation differs.

## Required formal work

Attempt a restricted proposition addressing:

**Under what conditions is a source representation sufficient for correct candidate-specific Bayesian evidence updates, and when can a limited downstream computation still produce systematic sign errors?**

Distinguish:

- Statistical sufficiency.
- Computational accessibility.
- Architectural expressivity.
- Training identifiability.

Construct counterexamples where the same sufficient information produces different signs because of different downstream operators.

# 12. NINTH TASK: TEST THE LEARNING-DYNAMICS EXPLANATION

Investigation VI found an interesting pattern.

Across transfer training, P-count models became closer to Bayes on average, while their R1 perfect-agreement deficit deepened.

This is not well explained by uniform error shrinkage.

But it is compatible with optimizing common cases while preserving or reinforcing a rare conditional error.

Investigate:

- When the wrong R1 sign first appears.
- Whether it is present before training layer 2.
- Whether it develops through attention, MLP, or readout changes.
- Whether the R1 gradient conflicts with more common training examples.
- Whether R1 examples are too rare to shape the downstream solution.
- Whether T1 pretraining provides a different initialization of the relevant computation.
- Whether targeted data balancing changes the learned operation.

## Important distinction

A training gradient pointing in one direction does not prove the optimizer will converge to the corresponding mechanism.

A rare event is not automatically underlearned.

Demonstrate the relevant learning-dynamics relationship using controlled interventions.

Where possible, use matched training streams and causal changes to the loss composition.

Do not retrospectively explain the error solely by the R1 prevalence.

### A particularly useful experiment

Compare models trained with:

- Original natural sampling.
- Moderately increased representation of R1.
- Balanced representation of the R1/R2/R3 regimes.
- Matched additional training without reweighting.

Keep total training compute comparable.

Determine whether a more balanced objective:

1. Corrects R1.
2. Preserves correct R2/R3 behavior.
3. Changes the same computation identified by the mechanistic analysis.
4. Generalizes to naturally distributed held-out episodes.

A correction obtained simply by memorizing the synthetic R1 criterion is less informative than one obtained through a reusable conditional evidence-update computation.

# 17. SCIENTIFIC OUTCOMES TO DISTINGUISH

Do not make one binary verdict hide a complex result.

### Outcome A — Frozen information is insufficient or inaccessible

Evidence shows that P-count's frozen state does not support the correct R1 response under specified, appropriately controlled downstream computations.

The result must distinguish demonstrated information insufficiency from limited reader capacity or unsuccessful optimization.

### Outcome B — The information is sufficient, but downstream learning fails

Controlled readers can recover the correct response from P-count representations, but the original transfer-trained solution learns the wrong conditional update.

This supports an interpretation or learning problem rather than a fundamental representation-loss explanation.

### Outcome C — The inherited readout is causally important

Changing the inherited readout or normalization eliminates or substantially changes the error under matched conditions.

The result must account for any changes in optimization or general task quality.

### Outcome D — A specific computational operation causes the error

A causally identified attention, MLP, readout, or combined operation produces the negative R1 update.

Interventions on that operation have predicted, selective effects.

This would be stronger than A–C alone.

### Outcome E — Selective correction succeeds

A previously specified intervention corrects R1 on new independent models while preserving R2/R3 and other important behavior.

Matched generic baselines do not explain away the intervention's claimed specificity.

If the correction works but its causal explanation remains unidentified, report behavioral rescue without claiming a mechanism.

### Outcome F — Generic training explains the phenomenon

Ordinary additional training, balanced data, or a standard adapter corrects R1 as effectively as the proposed mechanistic intervention.

This would favor a learning-dynamics explanation over a distinctive causal-repair method.

### Outcome G — The available evidence cannot identify the cause

Several mechanisms remain compatible with the observed intervention results.

Provide explicit alternative computational constructions or experimentally grounded indistinguishability arguments.

This is a valid scientific finding.

The final report should say which outcomes are supported and which remain unresolved.
