## Central research question

**Why does increasing the number of sources needed to determine trust dramatically delay, or prevent within the observed training budget, the acquisition of a mathematically valuable relational decision rule?**

Investigation XVI must distinguish three possibilities.

**Explanation A — Slower acquisition**

The higher-order relationship can be learned using the existing architecture and training procedure, but requires more effective optimization.

**Explanation B — Optimization barrier**

The higher-order relationship is representable, but the tested optimization process often fails to discover or exploit it, even under substantially extended training.

**Explanation C — Competing strategies and learning signals**

The timing of relational learning depends on the decision value of the relationship, the attractiveness of simpler competing rules, and the optimization signals associated with each.

These explanations are not necessarily mutually exclusive.

Your task is to produce experiments that distinguish their contributions.

Do not merely collect further demonstrations that high-order tasks are difficult.

# 1. WHAT INVESTIGATION XV ESTABLISHED

XV constructed four GATE variants using:

- Trust gate arity 2 or 3.
- Two levels of gate decision value.
- Four independently initialized models per condition.
- Mathematically matched decision value across arity.

The relevant mathematical references were verified independently.

The original task family had:

- Minimum trust-source count equal to gate arity \(a\).
- Minimum decision interaction order \(a+1\).
- Approximately 0.277 nats of gate benefit in the higher-value conditions.
- Approximately 0.138–0.140 nats in the lower-value conditions.

The experiment found:

### Complexity result

All eight arity-2 models acquired the complete gate.

Only two of eight arity-3 models acquired it within the 4,000-step budget.

Arity-2 acquisition occurred around steps 500–1,500.

The two successful arity-3 acquisitions occurred around steps 2,250–2,500.

The six unsuccessful arity-3 models remained near the gate-free optimum.

The learning-rate schedule had substantially decayed by the later training stages.

**Therefore, the experiment did not determine whether those six models were incapable of acquiring the gate or simply lacked sufficient effective training.**

### Decision-value result

At arity 2, the lower-value gate appeared to be acquired later in all four paired comparisons.

However, changing the generator parameter also altered the competing agreement cue.

The independent effect of decision value was not established.

### Learning-stage result

The frozen classifier favored a complexity-first learning sequence.

But the result was sensitive to stage thresholds, and in the arity-3 conditions the apparent intermediate stage was partly a consequence of the gate never being acquired.

Do not promote this into a general law.

### Mechanistic result

The proposed gradient-signal estimator failed its null-control test.

Its apparent correlation with learning difficulty was withdrawn.

Whether relational information is newly represented at the learning transition or merely becomes usable also remains unresolved.

### Cross-task result

Four independently initialized DeepSets models learned T4 from scratch and exhibited a source-based phase followed by acquisition of cross-question information.

This is useful evidence about staged relational learning, but does not establish a shared causal learning mechanism.

# 3. WORKSTREAM A — LATER, OR NOT ACQUIRED UNDER EXTENDED OPTIMIZATION?

**Highest priority. Mandatory.**

## A1. Establish the exact ambiguity

The unsuccessful XV arity-3 models stopped at the gate-free decision rule.

A failure at step 4,000 under a decayed learning-rate schedule does not establish an inherent representational or computational inability.

Likewise, successful acquisition after continuing training does not automatically establish that the only missing ingredient was training duration.

The learning-rate schedule, effective parameter displacement, optimizer state, data exposure, and available gradient signal all matter.

Construct a controlled comparison that separates them as far as possible.

## A2. Compare learning schedules

Investigate at least three meaningful conditions:

**Condition A — Original schedule**

The XV decayed learning-rate schedule and its existing results.

**Condition B — Extended constant-rate training**

Train with an appropriately selected constant learning rate for a substantially longer budget, targeting approximately 15,000–20,000 steps.

The constant rate must be calibrated on development runs for numerical stability and learning competence.

**Condition C — Continuation or learning-rate restart**

Continue previously unsuccessful XV models with a restored or controlled learning rate.

Use this to distinguish the effect of additional effective optimization from the effect of restarting the learning process under a different schedule.

Where scientifically useful, include a schedule-matched continuation that does not restart the learning rate.

The original, constant-rate, and continuation conditions answer different questions.

Do not collapse them into a single "more training" result.

## A3. Fresh-model replication

Use independent fresh model initializations for the principal constant-rate comparison.

Target at least four fresh models for each primary arity-3 condition, preferably covering both decision-value levels.

Because long training is expensive, calculate the actual feasibility before finalizing the confirmation design.

If resources cannot support both arity-3 value levels with four fresh runs each, prioritize a single mathematically justified arity-3 condition with at least four fresh seeds, and use the previously trained XV models for the continuation comparison.

Do not pretend that multiple continuations of the same trained checkpoint are independent fresh-model replications.

Use paired initializations and data streams only as secondary variance-reduction comparisons.

Report the independent random initialization count separately.

## A4. Longer training and checkpoint density

Target the 15,000–20,000-step range for the primary extended-training runs, unless the measured compute budget requires a narrower preregistered design.

Record diagnostic trajectories densely throughout training.

The later stages are particularly important.

XV's 250-step late checkpoint spacing was inadequate to determine the abruptness of some arity-3 transitions.

Use a regular diagnostic cadence sufficient to distinguish:

- No gate acquisition.
- Slow gradual acquisition.
- A relatively abrupt transition.
- Temporary gate use followed by regression.
- Stable acquired competence.

A 25–50-step diagnostic interval is preferable near suspected transitions.

Separate lightweight diagnostic snapshots from full optimizer checkpoints to control archive size.

Do not infer a sharp transition from observations separated by several hundred steps.

## A5. Diagnose censoring correctly

For each model, record:

- First reliable relational response.
- First sustained gate acquisition.
- Final relational competence.
- Time spent near the gate-free optimum.
- Training loss.
- Decision accuracy.
- Gradient and optimizer behavior.
- Any temporary regressions.
- Maximum completed training exposure.

Treat models that never acquire the gate as **right-censored observations**.

Never assign them an acquisition time equal to the final training step.

Use suitable acquisition-time analyses, such as survival-style summaries with appropriate small-sample uncertainty.

If acquisition remains rare, avoid estimating a precise mean learning time.

Report acquisition probability by the tested budget instead.

## A6. Compare effective optimization exposure

Training steps alone are not sufficient.

Report:

- Cumulative learning-rate exposure.
- Optimizer state behavior.
- Gradient norms.
- Update magnitudes.
- Candidate-relational response over time.
- Differences between continuing and restarting training.

An informative result would distinguish:

- The original schedule stopped making effective progress.
- A constant schedule permits later acquisition.
- A restart specifically unlocks acquisition.
- Extended optimization remains unsuccessful despite substantial updates.

Do not attribute causation to any one explanation without appropriate controls.

## A7. Rule out trivial incapacity

The gate is mathematically representable under the tested architecture only if its actual model implementation has sufficient expressive capacity.

Investigate this using relevant existing models and controlled trainability checks.

Possible evidence includes:

- Successful models of the same architecture.
- Explicit or constructive representability arguments.
- Diagnostic supervised feature supply.
- Controlled interventions that make gate information available.

Distinguish:

- Mathematical impossibility.
- Architectural expressivity limitations.
- Optimization failure.
- Limited training budget.

Do not claim absolute inability merely because 20,000 steps were insufficient.

## A8. Required Workstream A outcome

Classify the result as:

**A1 — Extended learning succeeds broadly:** The previously censored behavior is largely attributable to insufficient effective optimization under the original schedule.

**A2 — Extended learning succeeds selectively:** There is substantial seed-dependent acquisition variation or an optimization barrier.

**A3 — Extended learning remains rare:** The tested procedures do not reliably acquire the higher-order relation within the extended budget.

**A4 — Inconclusive:** The comparisons or resources are insufficient to distinguish the explanations.

Provide the actual model counts, acquisition times, uncertainty, and schedule comparisons.

Do not force a binary "later" or "never" conclusion.

# 4. WORKSTREAM B — ISOLATE DECISION VALUE FROM COMPETING AGREEMENT

**Mandatory. Second priority.**

The main decision-value experiment in XV was confounded.

Review A identified a way to improve it by controlling the reliabilities of different source components independently.

This is the most important mathematical correction for XVI.

## B1. Construct a two-reliability generator

Extend the GATE family so that the reliability of the paired evidence and the reliability of the single-source alternative can be varied independently.

Preserve the intended gate relation and task semantics.

The resulting generator should allow meaningful changes in the value of the gate without automatically strengthening or weakening the competing agreement rule in the same way.

Do not assume the parameterization succeeds merely because two reliability parameters exist.

Prove or verify what each parameter actually changes.

## B2. Verify the reference mathematics

For each candidate parameter setting, calculate:

- Bayes-optimal negative log-likelihood.
- Bayes-optimal accuracy.
- Gate-free optimal performance.
- Order-restricted optimal performance.
- Gate-specific decision benefit.
- Agreement-only decision benefit.
- Source-only performance.
- Source marginals.
- Gate-source mutual information.
- Competing cue overlap.
- Relevant gradient-signal properties.

The previous mathematical review suggested that approximately a 1.65-fold difference in gate value might be obtained while keeping the competing cue fixed.

**Verify that claim using the actual generator. Do not treat 1.65 as guaranteed.**

Find two parameter settings that produce a meaningful gate-value contrast while closely matching the competing cue.

Predefine tolerances.

If the match is imperfect, quantify the residual differences rather than declaring the confound eliminated.

## B3. Preserve relational complexity

For the primary value contrast, use the same gate arity.

Prefer arity 2 because XV showed reliable gate acquisition there.

This should make acquisition-time differences measurable instead of censoring nearly every run.

Verify that the mathematical interaction order remains constant across the chosen variants.

Confirm that neither condition introduces a low-order shortcut to the gate.

## B4. Freeze a decision-value prediction

Design an experiment capable of distinguishing:

**Benefit-driven learning:** The higher-value gate is acquired earlier or more reliably.

**Competition-driven learning:** Acquisition is primarily governed by the strength of the simpler competing strategy.

**Optimization-signal learning:** Acquisition is better explained by changes in learnable gradient information than by Bayes-level decision gain alone.

The strongest experiment would hold the competing cue nearly fixed while changing gate benefit, and then measure which learning behavior changes.

If the remaining changes in gradient signal prevent causal separation of the competing explanations, state this explicitly.

A clean decision-value contrast is not automatically a clean gradient-level contrast.

## B5. Fresh confirmation training

Use at least four genuinely independent fresh model initializations per primary value condition.

Prefer paired seed identifiers as an additional analysis, not the sole source of inferential independence.

Use the same architecture, training budget, input structure, and evaluation procedure across the matched value conditions.

Measure the full trajectories.

Do not evaluate only final accuracy.

The primary outcome is the time to sustained gate acquisition.

Secondary outcomes include:

- Intermediate agreement reliance.
- Source-only performance.
- Gate-free NLL.
- Transition duration.
- Final Bayes gap.
- Censored acquisition rate.

## B6. Required conclusion

Determine whether changing decision benefit at fixed relational complexity and controlled competing-cue strength causally changes the acquisition distribution.

If it does, report the effect size and uncertainty.

If it does not, report what effect sizes can reasonably be excluded.

If the generator remains confounded, report that failure as a mathematical limitation of the design.

Do not rescue a failed value prediction by redefining the usefulness measure after confirmation.

# 6. REBUILD THE LEARNING MEASUREMENTS

**Mandatory for all three workstreams.**

Several XV mechanism and stage measurements failed their controls.

Do not carry those measurements forward unchanged.

## D1. Cue-conditioned probes

XV initially interpreted certain probe responses as gate information, but the mathematical review showed that the probes were responding to the competing agreement cue.

For every relevant probe:

- Calculate its expected response under the agreement-only model.
- Calculate its expected response under the complete gate model.
- Measure excess gate information beyond the competing cue.
- Check performance on data that separates the gate from agreement.
- Use held-out inputs and checkpoints.

Do not use an arbitrary chance value of 0.5 when the correct conditional baseline is higher.

Distinguish:

- Decoding the agreement cue.
- Decoding the gate.
- Decoding evidence correlated with the gate.
- Causal use of the gate.

## D2. Floor-conditioned stage definitions

A model near the best gate-free optimum should not automatically be credited with an intermediate relational stage merely because it has not learned the full gate.

Define the lower-stage and intermediate-stage criteria using mathematically justified performance floors.

Require evidence of actual incremental use of the relevant intermediate relation.

A censored higher-order learner must not produce an automatic confirmation of complexity-first staging.

## D3. Confidence-dependent diagnostic calibration

XV's G and agreement-response diagnostics were not calibration-free.

Their magnitudes could change with output confidence.

Correct this by:

- Measuring ideal-reference responses at matched confidence.
- Including confidence-sensitive null models.
- Separating changes in candidate decision from output sharpening.
- Reporting properly normalized intervention effects where possible.

A sudden increase in output confidence is not by itself proof of new relational computation.

## D4. Dense transition measurements

Use sufficiently frequent snapshots throughout the full period in which gate learning may occur.

Report:

- Time of first measurable acquisition.
- Time of stable acquisition.
- Transition width.
- Confidence and calibration changes.
- Intermediate-relation use.
- Final task competence.

Do not call a transition abrupt when checkpoint spacing is too coarse to distinguish a gradual process.

## D5. Rebuild the learning-signal estimator

The XV gradient-SNR estimator returned values near its purported signal level even when the true signal was absent.

It is invalid for the intended scientific claim.

Construct a corrected estimator with:

- Exact or verified zero-signal controls.
- Positive-signal controls.
- Noise-calibrated reference values.
- Repeated estimates for variance.
- Explicit mathematical interpretation.

Do not fit it to XV's known complexity labels and then claim independent validation.

If no reliable estimator can be built within the research budget, leave the learning-signal hypothesis untested.

This is preferable to reporting a misleading correlation.
