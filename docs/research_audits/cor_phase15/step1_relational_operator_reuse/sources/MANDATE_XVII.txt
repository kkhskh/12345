# 1. WHY XVII EXISTS

Investigation XVI revealed a potentially important phenomenon that was not part of its original frozen predictions.

The six question positions in a GATE Transformer share the same underlying model parameters, yet they do not necessarily acquire relational reasoning simultaneously.

In particular, model `C_s202` exhibited a long partially acquired state.

For approximately 1,600 training steps, the model used the trust relationship successfully at three question positions while remaining on a simpler strategy at the other three.

The acquired positions subsequently expanded to include the remaining positions.

This means a network can display different relational abilities at different positions despite substantial parameter sharing.

But the interpretation is unresolved.

A single network can potentially implement:

1. A reusable relational computation.
2. A shared computation accessed differently by each position.
3. Distinct position-conditioned implementations using the same shared weights.

These are not equivalent.

**The primary mission of XVII is to distinguish them experimentally.**

The secondary mission is to determine whether any of these mechanisms are predicted by COR's original mathematical framework.

Do not assume they are.

# 3. THREE COMPETING NEURAL MECHANISMS

**Central theoretical framework of XVII.**

Formulate the following competing explanations as precise causal hypotheses.

## H1 — Shared and uniformly reusable computation

The network develops a relational operation that can be used across question positions without requiring position-specific learned access.

The operation should transfer between positions when the relevant relational inputs are correctly aligned.

Position differences may arise from temporary optimization or performance differences, but the same abstract computation is already available through comparable pathways.

## H2 — Shared computation with position-dependent access

The model develops an internal relational computation that is potentially available to multiple positions.

However, different question positions learn different ways of accessing, routing, activating, or reading out that computation.

A position may fail behaviorally because it does not yet use an existing computation, not because the computation is absent everywhere.

A successful causal intervention on the relevant access mechanism should restore relational behavior without transferring the correct answer or copying the donor's source evidence.

## H3 — Distinct position-conditioned implementations

The shared network weights support different effective computations at different positions.

These may involve different attention paths, activation subspaces, feature interactions, or conditional circuits.

A learned computation from one position may not transfer to another without replacing additional implementation-specific components.

The model need not contain separate parameter sets.

Distinct computations can be expressed through shared weights conditioned on question embeddings and contextual attention patterns.

## H4 — Other or non-identifiable mechanisms

The actual network may combine these explanations.

A shared subcomputation may feed distinct downstream operations.

Some positions may share mechanisms while others do not.

Multiple reparameterizations may explain the same interventions.

**Do not force every model into H1, H2, or H3 if the evidence is insufficient.**

The investigation should distinguish them wherever possible and report non-identifiability where necessary.

# 5. EXPERIMENT A — POSITION AND QUESTION-EMBEDDING SWAPS

**Mandatory.**

The architecture uses shared weights across question positions.

Question embeddings and position-dependent attention may affect how the shared weights are used.

Test whether relational ability follows:

- The question embedding.
- The actual question content.
- The physical position.
- The surrounding attention context.
- Some interaction among these factors.

## 5.1 Controlled interventions

At partially acquired checkpoints, select:

- A position that has acquired the gate.
- A position that has not acquired the gate.

Construct controlled tests involving:

**A. Embedding interchange**

Swap or transplant the corresponding learned question embeddings under a precisely defined intervention.

**B. Content interchange**

Exchange question content while preserving the appropriate underlying relational labels and source structure.

**C. Position interchange**

Change where equivalent questions appear, with the mathematical task held fixed.

**D. Joint intervention**

Swap content and question embeddings together when needed to preserve a meaningful task interpretation.

## 5.2 Essential controls

For every intervention, verify:

- The correct target answer.
- The gate relation.
- The source distribution.
- The model's competence on the corresponding unmodified examples.
- The absence of answer leakage.
- Whether the intervention changes the semantics of the question.
- Whether it creates a configuration never encountered during training.

Include matched controls that change embeddings or positions without transferring a learned relational ability.

Use several donor-recipient pairs rather than a single convenient example.

## 5.3 Discriminating outcomes

A result where relational ability follows the embedding supports position-conditioned access or computation.

It does **not**, by itself, distinguish the two.

A result where the ability follows question content supports a different interpretation.

A result where it remains tied to the physical slot supports position-dependent routing or contextual processing.

Derive the detailed predictions before interpreting the outcomes.

Do not equate a successful embedding swap with proof of a shared relational operator.

# 6. EXPERIMENT B — CAUSAL TRANSFER OF RELATIONAL COMPUTATION

**Highest-priority causal experiment.**

Determine whether a position that already uses the gate can supply a causally relevant computation to a position that has not acquired it.

## 6.1 Distinguish what is transferred

Three different things could be transferred by an activation patch.

**Source evidence**

The donor may carry useful answer-bearing values.

**Access or routing**

The donor may provide an attention configuration or activation state that permits the recipient to use a relational computation already available elsewhere.

**Relational computation**

The donor may provide an abstract gate-dependent computation that the recipient can reuse.

These explanations must not be conflated.

## 6.2 Essential donor controls

Construct donor-recipient pairs that distinguish relational state from ordinary source content.

Include:

**Relation-preserving donors**

Change the gate-defining source tokens while preserving the gate's value.

Measure the response to these changes before using the donor in a causal transfer claim.

**Evidence-matched donors**

Match answer-bearing evidence as closely as mathematically possible while varying the gate relation.

Document which input values can and cannot be matched simultaneously.

**Gate-matched, evidence-different donors**

Preserve the relational state while changing other source evidence.

**Non-relational control donors**

Use appropriately matched activation sources that do not carry the intended relational computation.

**Wrong-gate donors**

Use donors whose relational state predicts an incompatible gate response.

Every proposed donor type must be checked for mathematical feasibility under the actual generator.

Do not invent impossible source combinations or use off-support states without labeling them.

## 6.3 Compare multiple interventions

Test:

- Full residual-stream patches.
- Restricted activation subspace patches.
- Attention-row interchange.
- Attention-pattern interchange.
- Selected query/key effects where justified.
- Selected value-path effects where justified.
- Access-path interventions suggested by Experiment A.

Do not assume that residual transfer and attention-pattern transfer test the same mechanism.

A successful full residual patch may simply move useful evidence.

A successful attention patch may change evidence selection rather than transfer a reusable relational computation.

Identify the information each intervention can carry.

## 6.4 Necessary negative controls

Include:

- Same-location sham patches.
- Off-target patches.
- Random matched subspaces.
- Source-preserving decoys.
- Norm-matched perturbations.
- Relation-preserving counterfactuals.
- Interventions that carry equivalent ordinary source evidence without the target relational state.
- Reverse-direction transfer.

Measure whether the intervention causes a specific gate-dependent change beyond its matched control.

A simple patched-versus-unpatched comparison is insufficient.

## 6.5 Quantitative success criteria

The central outcome is not whether patching increases accuracy.

The outcome is whether a donor position's relational computation can be transferred to an unlearned position in a manner that:

1. Follows the appropriate counterfactual gate relation.
2. Preserves unrelated answer-bearing evidence.
3. Survives source-matched controls.
4. Depends on the proposed causal feature or route.
5. Transfers across multiple donor-recipient pairs.
6. Produces the mathematically predicted decision changes.
7. Cannot be explained adequately by a simpler evidence-transfer mechanism.

Separate behavioral sufficiency from mechanistic identity.

Even strong transfer does not by itself establish that the network uses a unique internal operator.

# 7. EXPERIMENT C — SHARED COMPUTATION OR SHARED ACCESS?

**Mandatory.**

Use the strongest observations from Experiments A and B to identify where the acquired relational operation resides and how each position uses it.

Investigate:

- Whether learned positions rely on the same activation directions.
- Whether position-specific transformations align these directions.
- Whether the same attention heads play equivalent causal roles.
- Whether the same readout process can use the relational information across positions.
- Whether different positions require different conditional routes.
- Whether a common intermediate computation is present even when the final decision is not gate-sensitive.

## 7.1 Cross-position causal alignment

Compare same-position and cross-position interventions using properly matched inputs.

Test whether a causal feature learned for one position generalizes to another without retraining.

If alignment transformations are necessary, determine whether they are:

- Fixed mathematically.
- Learned using independent development data.
- Position-specific.
- Identifiable from the available observations.

Do not fit arbitrary alignment maps using confirmation outcomes and then claim prospective transfer.

## 7.2 Access-specific interventions

Attempt to restore gate use at an unlearned slot by intervening on its access path while keeping the recipient's own evidence intact.

The important question is:

**Can the recipient use an existing relational computation once its access mechanism is changed?**

If so, identify exactly what was changed.

A shared computation with position-dependent access should produce a different intervention pattern from a genuinely position-specific computation.

Predefine the expected differences.

## 7.3 Necessity tests

Where a shared component is proposed, test whether disrupting it affects gate use across multiple learned positions.

Where a position-specific component is proposed, test whether disrupting it selectively affects the corresponding position.

Use matched perturbations and competence controls.

Avoid relying on decodability as proof of use.

## 7.4 Identifiability

State which explanations are observationally equivalent under the available interventions.

If two mechanisms cannot be distinguished, report the equivalence rather than choosing the more appealing interpretation.

# 8. EXPERIMENT D — DOES LEARNING AT ONE POSITION HELP THE OTHERS?

**Mandatory if a valid and computationally affordable intervention is established.**

The previous experiments examine a trained model's internal computation.

This experiment examines how the computation is acquired.

## 8.1 Central question

When a model learns to use a relationship at one position, does that make it easier for other positions to learn the same relationship?

If learning is driven by a reusable computation, improvement at one position might benefit others.

If learning is dominated by position-specific access, progress at one position may not immediately generalize.

If position-conditioned implementations are largely separate, targeted training may produce strongly localized effects.

These are hypotheses, not automatic consequences.

## 8.2 Controlled training interventions

Develop a carefully matched intervention that increases learning pressure at one position during the partially acquired stage.

Possible approaches include:

- Position-targeted training examples.
- Position-targeted loss weighting.
- Controlled position-specific access interventions.
- Restricted modifications to question embeddings.
- Causal training-pathway manipulations suggested by earlier results.

Choose the intervention using mathematical and development evidence.

## 8.3 Controls

Require:

- A matched amount of training.
- A matched total loss contribution where feasible.
- A sham intervention.
- An intervention targeting a different position.
- A non-relational control.
- Identical initialization and data streams where paired comparisons are scientifically appropriate.
- Independent fresh seeds for confirmation.

Track the possibility that changing one slot's loss also changes gradients through shared weights.

Do not assume position-targeted supervision produces position-local parameter changes.

## 8.4 Measure cross-position effects

Record:

- Acquisition timing in the targeted position.
- Acquisition timing in every untargeted position.
- Changes in the relational activation signature.
- Changes in attention routes.
- Transfer effects before and after training.
- Overall model competence.
- Whether training at one position causes interference elsewhere.

The decisive finding would be a predicted, controlled pattern of transfer or independence across positions.

Do not interpret a general improvement in accuracy as proof of operator sharing.

# 12. MATHEMATICAL AND CAUSAL CONTROLS

**Mandatory across the investigation.**

Every causal claim must survive appropriate controls.

## 12.1 Source matching

Distinguish transferred source evidence from transferred relational information.

The XVI residual-patching comparison lacked the necessary source-matched donor control.

XVII must fix this explicitly.

## 12.2 Relation-preserving edits

Use edits that change relevant tokens while preserving the mathematical relation, where supported by the generator.

These should establish whether a proposed causal variable depends on the abstract relation or on a specific encoding.

## 12.3 Counterfactual gate changes

Use changes that alter the gate while appropriately controlling other relevant evidence.

Derive the correct decision under each edited input.

Do not infer the expected answer from the model's own behavior.

## 12.4 Off-target and random controls

Use matched sham interventions and appropriately calibrated random subspaces.

A positive transfer effect is uninterpretable without relevant negative controls.

## 12.5 Calibration

Use the corrected decision-level measures from XVI.

Do not treat output-confidence changes as gate acquisition.

## 12.6 Causal necessity and sufficiency

Distinguish:

- Information being decodable.
- Information being available to downstream computation.
- Information being causally sufficient under intervention.
- Information being necessary for the observed decision.
- Information being part of a reusable abstract computation.

These require different evidence.

## 12.7 Mathematical ideal references

Evaluate the proposed interventions against exact GATE decision rules.

Do not interpret an intervention as successful if the corresponding ideal-reference behavior has not been established.

Where the ideal computation does not support a claimed diagnostic, reject that diagnostic.

- A shared computation.
