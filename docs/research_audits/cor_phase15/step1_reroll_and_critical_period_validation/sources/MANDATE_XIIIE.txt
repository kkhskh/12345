## Why this investigation is necessary

Investigation XIII-C claimed that the fresh readout-map initialization determined which internal decision algorithm a Transformer learned.

Investigation XIII-D falsified that explanation.

XIII-D found that outcomes depended on interactions involving the pretrained parent, fresh layer-2 initialization, and readout initialization. However, its own mechanism review identified a simpler unresolved possibility:

**Changing a seed may merely re-draw a random initialization and produce another sample from the distribution of possible trained solutions.**

There may be no special causal significance to matching the seed numbers of independently generated components.

The existing sample is too small to establish whether the apparent initialization combinations have a meaningful structure beyond this re-roll explanation.

At the same time:

- The proposed early critical period lacks sham masking, short-window controls, and replication.
- Several previous causal measurements were not compared with their ideal-reference values.
- The relationship among different sufficient subspaces has not been adequately characterized.
- Earlier scoring procedures allowed confirmation results to influence the planning of later frozen tests.

**These issues must be resolved before another extensive theory-building investigation.**

Do not use XIII-E to invent a new algorithm-selection mechanism unless the evidence genuinely requires one.

# 1. THREE MANDATORY RESEARCH OBJECTIVES

This investigation has exactly three primary scientific objectives.

### Objective A — Test the re-roll null hypothesis

Determine whether re-drawing a newly initialized component simply produces another sample from a parent-dependent distribution of neural solutions, or whether identifiable initialization structure influences the outcome.

### Objective B — Validate the early-training sensitivity result

Determine whether short pathway interventions have a reproducible, route-specific effect, rather than merely disturbing optimization at a sensitive time.

### Objective C — Repair causal measurement and interpretation

Establish which existing carrier, decoder, eligibility, and attention-row conclusions survive ideal-model reference calculations and properly matched controls.

These objectives must be completed before optional additional exploration.

# 2. WORKSTREAM A — THE RE-ROLL EXPERIMENT

**Highest priority. Mandatory.**

This is the principal investigation.

## A1. Define the competing explanations precisely

Consider:

**H0 — Re-roll explanation**

Re-drawing a fresh component results in another draw from a distribution of learned solutions, with no special role for the numerical matching of independently generated seed identifiers.

The probability of an early, mixture, or hybrid solution may depend on the pretrained parent and on which components are held fixed.

Do not assume all parents have the same solution probabilities.

**H1 — Initialization structure**

Particular relationships between the fresh readout map and fresh layer-2 initialization systematically influence which solution emerges.

This influence may be expressed through measurable alignment or other joint properties.

**H2 — Asymmetric solution stability**

Starting from an early-producing configuration versus a mixture-producing configuration changes the response to replacing one component in a way not adequately explained by random re-drawing.

**H3 — Higher-order interactions**

No single-component rule explains the outcome, but interactions involving parent, readout initialization, and layer-2 initialization contain reproducible predictive information.

These hypotheses must be separated carefully.

The statement "a fresh random draw changes the outcome" is not itself evidence for an identifiable selection mechanism.

Likewise, an initialization-dependent deterministic training process can generate an apparently random distribution of solutions.

## A2. Audit initialization independence

Before conducting new training, verify how the following are generated:

- Fresh layer-2 initialization, L.
- Fresh readout-map initialization, W.
- Training-data stream, D.

Check that the implementation truly generates the relevant tensors independently.

Inspect whether identical seed numbers introduce hidden statistical coupling or merely act as arbitrary labels for independent random draws.

Record:

- The actual random-number generators.
- Their seed transformations.
- Parameter tensor hashes.
- Initialization distributions.
- Any shared state.
- Determinism assumptions.

If seed matching creates no statistical dependence, document this mathematically and in code.

Do not use the phrase "matching seeds" as though it describes physical alignment unless such alignment is demonstrated.

## A3. Run the primary 64-run experiment

Select **four previously characterized diagonal configurations**, including:

- Two that produced early-readout solutions.
- Two that produced mixture solutions.
- At least two independently pretrained parents represented across the four configurations.

Use established XIII-B/C/D outcomes to select these development anchors, and disclose that selection.

For each anchor, run:

- Eight independent re-draws of W while holding L and D fixed.
- Eight independent re-draws of L while holding W and D fixed.

That yields:

**4 anchors × 16 re-draws = 64 controlled short fine-tuning runs.**

Evaluate every run at step 1211.

Use the same architecture, generator, learning schedule, and class measurements as the established experiments.

Report continuous early-readout and mixture-head measurements rather than relying only on categorical classifications.

The training commands, seed list, anchor selection, analysis definitions, and stopping criteria must be frozen before inspecting new outcomes.

## A4. Address anchor-selection bias

Because the anchors were selected partly by their known outcomes, simple comparisons can exhibit regression toward their underlying solution probabilities.

Do not interpret a change away from an early anchor as evidence of instability without considering this selection effect.

Construct the correct conditional comparisons.

Compare:

- W re-draws from early anchors.
- W re-draws from mixture anchors.
- L re-draws from early anchors.
- L re-draws from mixture anchors.
- Parent-specific baseline solution frequencies.

Use a clear statistical null model.

Distinguish differences explained by the component held fixed from differences requiring a special initialization interaction.

The original diagonal-versus-off-diagonal result was not statistically compelling. Do not treat it as a discovered law.

## A5. Confirm the meaning of step 1211

Step-1211 classification is a screening outcome, not automatically the final trained solution.

Preselect a balanced subset of the 64 runs and continue it to step 4000.

Include:

- Early classifications.
- Mixture classifications.
- Hybrid or borderline cases.
- Both replacement types.
- Both pretrained parents.

Where feasible, finish at least eight representative runs.

Do not choose the continuation subset only after seeing which results favor the preferred hypothesis.

Report class transitions from step 1211 to step 4000.

If step-1211 classification proves unreliable, account for that uncertainty in every conclusion based on the 64-run screen.

## A6. Analyze probabilities and uncertainty

Estimate, where supported:

- P(early | W re-draw).
- P(early | L re-draw).
- P(mixture | W re-draw).
- P(mixture | L re-draw).
- Parent-specific solution frequencies.
- Anchor-specific variation.
- Differences between early and mixture anchors.
- The frequency of hybrid outcomes.
- Stability of continuous implementation measurements.

Use binomial or multinomial uncertainty estimates appropriately.

Account for the shared pretrained parents and fixed anchor components.

Sixty-four runs do not constitute 64 independently pretrained models.

Do not use individual episodes as independent evidence about model-selection probabilities.

Avoid declaring equivalence merely because a hypothesis test does not reject a difference.

If the sample cannot distinguish the hypotheses, report an inconclusive result with an explicit bound on what remains possible.

## A7. Investigate initialization alignment only if warranted

If the primary experiment reveals repeatable structure, investigate whether it is associated with the relationship between W and layer-2 keys and queries.

Candidate measurements include:

- Readout-to-key alignment.
- Readout-to-query alignment.
- Relevant joint W–L projections.
- Initial candidate-output sensitivity.
- Early gradient alignment.

Use theory-guided measurements rather than a broad search for accidental correlations.

Develop an explicit prediction.

Test it on held-out initialization draws.

Do not infer a mechanism from an association selected and evaluated on the same 64 runs.

If no convincing structure appears, do not create a complicated alignment story to rescue the hypothesis.

## A8. Required decision

At the end, classify the evidence into one of four outcomes:

**A — Evidence for structured selection:** A specified initialization relationship predicts new outcomes beyond appropriate re-roll baselines.

**B — Evidence consistent with re-roll:** The outcomes are statistically compatible with the parent-conditional re-draw model, with sufficiently informative bounds on plausible structured effects.

**C — Mixed:** Some parents or components show structure while others do not.

**D — Inconclusive:** The data are insufficient to distinguish the explanations.

Report the actual estimates, uncertainty, and model comparisons.

**Do not interpret outcome B as proof that neural training contains no deeper algorithm-selection mechanism.**

It means that this experimental setting has not supported the particular low-order mechanism being tested.

# 3. WORKSTREAM B — MAKE THE CRITICAL-PERIOD RESULT RIGOROUS

**Second priority. Mandatory.**

XIII-D found that blocking either route during the first 100 training steps could permanently redirect the final organization.

However:

- Only one diagnostic run was available per direction.
- No sham mask was run.
- No intervention shorter than 100 steps was tested.
- Later interventions were not matched for severity.
- One pathway intervention caused much stronger loss disruption than the other.

The current evidence establishes sensitivity to early perturbation, not a fully identified critical period.

## B1. Sham-mask control

Construct a sham intervention that matches the real masking procedure in relevant implementation and optimization details but does not specifically block either hypothesized information route.

A suitable control should account for:

- Intervention duration.
- Execution pathway.
- Numerical operations.
- Gradient and activation disruption, where relevant.
- Random-number consumption.
- Training-data exposure.

Verify that the sham does not unknowingly block a meaningful alternative route.

Distinguish a genuine neutral sham from a general-disturbance control.

If a perfectly neutral sham cannot be constructed, use separate sham and disturbance controls and report what each establishes.

## B2. Short intervention windows

Test both route restrictions using:

- 25 training steps.
- 50 training steps.
- 100 training steps.

Use at least three independently specified data-order seeds per condition when the computational budget permits.

Include corresponding no-mask baselines and sham interventions.

Reuse initialization tensors across the paired conditions.

Keep the total training schedule unchanged.

Do not count a failed-to-train model as evidence that a successful alternative algorithm emerged.

## B3. Severity-matched late intervention

The earlier late-block comparison was confounded because the two blocked pathways experienced very different loss increases.

Construct late interventions matched on a scientifically meaningful disturbance measure, such as:

- Immediate loss increase.
- Candidate-accuracy disruption.
- Relevant activation or gradient displacement.

Prespecify the matching method using development data.

Avoid tuning the intervention severity directly on confirmation outcomes.

Test whether a comparable disturbance later in training can still change the final implementation.

If a late disturbance changes the solution, the interpretation of a uniquely early critical period must be revised.

## B4. Measure persistent consequences

For every condition, record:

- Final competence.
- Continuous early-readout strength.
- Mixture-head formation.
- Route-specific information.
- Final decision accuracy.
- Loss trajectory.
- Whether the intervention's effect persists after release.

Also measure whether the unused route retains relational information.

Where appropriate, test whether readout-only retraining can reactivate that information.

Do not automatically call retained information an available backup; determine whether it can actually be used.

## B5. Separate competing interpretations

Compare:

**Route-specific early selection**

A particular information pathway being unavailable early changes which solution develops.

**General early sensitivity**

Many disturbances early in optimization can redirect the solution.

**Passive learning commitment**

The network learns one useful route first and later stops using alternatives.

**Active competition**

One pathway causally suppresses the development or use of another.

These explanations may coexist.

Sham controls and temporal interventions may distinguish route-specific sensitivity from general disturbance, but may not by themselves distinguish active competition from commitment.

Do not claim more than the experiment establishes.

## B6. Mandatory conclusion

Determine whether:

- The 100-step finding replicates.
- Shorter blocks produce a similar effect.
- Sham disturbances reproduce the effect.
- Severity-matched late disturbances produce similar effects.
- The resulting models remain competent.
- There is evidence for a specifically early route-selection window.

If results are borderline, report uncertainty and avoid a binary declaration.

A rigorous negative result is acceptable.
