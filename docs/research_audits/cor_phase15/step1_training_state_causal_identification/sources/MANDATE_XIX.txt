# 1. THE SCIENTIFIC PROBLEM LEFT BY XVIII

Investigation XIV established that temporarily blocking a selected pathway during the first 100 training steps can change the eventual organization of a T4 Transformer.

Investigation XVIII attempted to identify which component of that block caused the effect.

It separated several intervention components:

- Forward information carried through the targeted route.
- Backward gradients associated with the targeted route.
- Attention allocation to the route.
- Learning of the underlying parameters.

The results were informative but did **not** isolate a mechanism.

## 1.1 What XVIII actually established

The frozen primary experiment had only two evaluable baseline pairs.

Six of eight frozen hypotheses were contradicted under its reported rule, while two remained inconclusive. Under a different reasonable reading of an ambiguous validity rule, fewer contradictions survive.

The strongest positive findings came from the corrected sensitivity analysis, not the primary frozen verdict.

In that sensitivity analysis:

**Forward-content destruction**

Destroying information transmitted through the targeted route while allowing attention allocation to remain active changed the learned organization in five of five evaluable pairs.

But the intervention also stopped relevant value gradients.

Therefore, forward-content destruction was not isolated from altered value learning.

**All-gradient intervention**

Removing the relevant backward learning contribution while leaving the forward pass intact changed the learned route organization on the mixture base, and changed route necessity without a corresponding mixture-like behavioral change on the early base.

But gradient contributions through shared parameters and complementary routes remained possible.

**Routing-gradient intervention**

Removing the targeted routing-gradient contribution produced different effects in early and mixture organizations, including stable hybrids.

This did not establish that routing gradients alone were responsible.

**Attention-share clamp**

Holding forward attention allocation near its initial level while allowing the route to continue learning did not produce the organizational switch.

However, the underlying route remained trainable, and its latent strength could rebound after release.

**Graded suppression**

Strong partial suppression also changed the learned organization, demonstrating that a mathematically complete block is not always required.

However, this simultaneously reduced forward contribution and associated gradients.

## 1.2 What remains unidentified

The results are compatible with several explanations:

1. Information-dependent parameter learning.
2. Competition between learned routes.
3. Changes in value-processing parameters.
4. Changes in attention-routing parameters.
5. Downstream adaptation to altered evidence.
6. Changes in optimizer state.
7. Interactions among several learned parameter groups.
8. General optimization effects.

No single explanation has been causally established.

The previously proposed statement that the route switches when it receives no useful learning signal was withdrawn after review.

**Do not resurrect that explanation under different wording without a discriminating experiment.**

# 2. A FUNDAMENTAL CHANGE IN EXPERIMENTAL STRATEGY

Previous investigations primarily manipulated attention or gradients during training and examined the resulting network.

XIX must additionally investigate the **learned state produced by those interventions**.

The main object is now:

\[
S_t = (\theta_t, m_t, v_t, \ldots)
\]

where:

- \(\theta_t\) denotes model parameters at training step \(t\).
- \(m_t, v_t\) denote relevant optimizer state, including Adam moments.
- Additional state variables must be included if the training procedure actually uses them.

Use the exact optimizer definition implemented in the repository.

Let:

- \(S_{100}^{B}\) denote the state after 100 steps of baseline training.
- \(S_{100}^{I}\) denote the state after the same 100 steps with the targeted intervention.

Both training trajectories must start from the same initialization and use matched data streams and randomness.

The primary question becomes:

**Which components of the difference between these states are necessary or sufficient to change the later learned organization?**

The difference is a consequence of the entire training intervention.

It must not automatically be interpreted as a uniquely identifiable local causal mechanism.

Parameter changes are correlated, shared across functions, and can interact nonlinearly.

The experimental design must address those facts directly.

# 4. WORKSTREAM A — PARAMETER-STATE TRANSPLANTATION

**Principal new experiment.**

The central question is whether the difference between the baseline and intervened training states causes the eventual change in learned organization.

## A1. Establish matched trajectories

For each eligible baseline model, create two training trajectories:

**B — Baseline**

Train normally for the first 100 steps.

**I — Intervention**

Train from exactly the same initial state and on the same data stream while applying the validated pathway intervention for the first 100 steps.

Verify that all randomness except the intended intervention is matched.

At step 100, save:

- Complete model parameters.
- Complete optimizer state.
- Learning-rate schedule state.
- Relevant buffers.
- Training loss.
- Attention and causal-route diagnostics.
- Parameter-group update norms.

After step 100, remove the intervention from all continuation experiments.

## A2. Full-state interchange

Before dividing the parameters into groups, verify that full-state interchange behaves as expected.

Compare:

1. Baseline state continued normally.
2. Intervention state continued normally.
3. Baseline parameters with intervention optimizer state.
4. Intervention parameters with baseline optimizer state.
5. Full baseline state.
6. Full intervention state.

Some of these combinations may be off the natural training trajectory.

Their interpretations must be limited accordingly.

The full-state controls should show whether transplantation machinery is correctly implemented.

A full state transplant is not itself evidence of a particular mechanism.

## A3. Parameter-only and optimizer-only interventions

Separate, as far as operationally possible:

**Parameter effect**

What happens when the network receives the intervention-induced parameter state but retains the corresponding baseline optimizer state?

**Optimizer-history effect**

What happens when the model parameters remain at baseline values but the optimizer state is replaced?

**Joint effect**

Does transferring parameters and optimizer state together produce a different result from either transfer alone?

Include fresh optimizer-state and unmodified-state controls where scientifically appropriate.

Do not assume that optimizer-state-only transfer is a valid natural mediation intervention.

Treat unnatural parameter/optimizer combinations as synthetic causal tests and measure their instability.

## A4. Parameter-group transplantation

Investigate causal contributions from:

- Query/key attention parameters.
- Value/output attention parameters.
- Relevant attention heads where individual head separation is valid.
- Downstream MLP parameters.
- Readout parameters.
- Layer-normalization parameters where relevant.
- Other groups identified by the actual gradient audit.

Use the real model structure.

Do not invent artificial independent modules that do not exist.

For each component group \(G\), construct precisely defined interventions such as:

\[
\theta^{B \leftarrow I}_G
\]

meaning that the baseline state receives the corresponding parameter values from the intervened state, with other parameters retained from baseline.

Also test the reverse direction.

## A5. Test necessity and sufficiency

A credible parameter-level causal claim requires more than one transplant direction.

Evaluate:

**Sufficiency**

Does replacing the baseline component with its intervention-trained counterpart cause the later organization to change?

**Necessity**

Does restoring the baseline component in the intervention-trained model prevent the organization change?

**Interaction**

Are two or more parameter groups jointly required?

**Robustness**

Does the effect persist under reasonable controls for perturbation magnitude and continued optimization?

A parameter group that is sufficient but not necessary may be one of several alternative causal routes.

Do not force unique attribution.

## A6. Include interactions

If no single parameter group reproduces the effect, test carefully selected combinations.

Examples include:

- Query/key plus value/output.
- Attention plus downstream MLP.
- Attention plus readout.
- Parameter transplant plus optimizer-state transplant.

Choose combinations based on a mathematical causal model and development results.

Do not conduct an uncontrolled combinatorial search and present the best combination as preregistered evidence.

## A7. State interpolation and perturbation controls

Simple parameter transplantation can create an unnatural combination of learned features.

Investigate whether the effect depends on:

- Large parameter jumps.
- Increased training loss.
- Incompatible residual representations.
- Disrupted optimizer moments.
- Destruction of task competence.

Use matched-norm random perturbations, within-trajectory swaps, and appropriate interpolation diagnostics.

Do not call a hybrid parameter state a naturally occurring neural mechanism merely because it produces the desired output.

# 5. WORKSTREAM B — SEPARATE FORWARD CONTENT FROM VALUE LEARNING

**Mandatory missing causal control.**

XVIII's `vrg` intervention simultaneously modified forward content and stopped associated value gradients.

This was the most important unresolved confound in the content-destruction result.

## B1. Implement the missing value-gradient arm

Investigate the proposed `vsg` intervention or its mathematically appropriate equivalent.

The objective is to alter the targeted value-learning contribution while preserving the intended forward computation and relevant routing-gradient contribution as closely as the computational graph allows.

Do not assume the name `vsg` establishes its meaning.

Verify it through gradient-path tests.

## B2. Reciprocal comparison

Construct a scientifically meaningful comparison between:

- Forward-content disruption with its direct learning consequences.
- Forward-content disruption with an explicitly controlled backward rule.
- Value-gradient suppression while preserving the forward computation.
- Routing-gradient suppression.
- Combined relevant gradient suppression.
- Untreated baseline.
- Full route block.

If certain combinations require synthetic gradients, describe them as such.

Do not claim they reproduce ordinary gradient descent.

## B3. Mathematical feasibility

Some forward and backward effects are coupled by the chain rule.

An intervention that holds the forward function fixed while independently changing part of the gradient is an intervention on the optimization algorithm.

Its interpretation is different from removing information from a normal neural network.

Explicitly separate:

- Natural forward perturbations.
- Natural-gradient consequences.
- Synthetic gradient interventions.
- Parameter freezes.
- Optimizer-state manipulation.

This distinction is mandatory.

## B4. Measured outcomes

Measure:

- Actual forward content.
- Attention share.
- Parameter-specific gradients.
- Parameter updates.
- Optimizer-state changes.
- Loss trajectory.
- Final causal organization.
- Final task competence.
- Functional evidence-combination behavior.

The experiment must distinguish whether the organizational effect follows changes in transmitted information, changes in learning updates, or their interaction.

If those causes remain inseparable, state that explicitly.

# 6. WORKSTREAM C — DIRECTLY TEST ROUTING-GRADIENT LEARNING

**Mandatory where the audited graph supports a meaningful separation.**

XVIII's `gsg` results differed by organizational base and produced stable hybrids.

But stopping gradients at selected attention logits did not necessarily remove every way the relevant routing parameters could learn.

## C1. Define routing-gradient contribution

Give an operational, testable definition of the learning contribution associated with the targeted route.

Specify the relevant paths through the computational graph.

Measure what fraction of the affected parameter updates is removed by each intervention.

Do not describe a partial gradient stop as complete removal of route learning.

## C2. Compare local and parameter-level intervention

Where feasible, compare:

- Edge-local backward suppression.
- Suppression of the route-associated contribution to parameter gradients.
- Relevant parameter-group freezing.
- Parameter-state transplantation of the learned routing changes.

These interventions have different side effects.

That difference must be measured rather than hidden.

## C3. Verify specificity

Check whether each intervention also changes:

- Complementary attention edges.
- Source-irrelevant edges.
- Other learned relational routes.
- Shared head-level parameters.
- Gradient flow into downstream blocks.

A causally persuasive result would identify which changes are required for the later organizational switch.

If freezing a shared parameter changes several routes, it cannot be presented as an isolated single-route intervention.

# 7. WORKSTREAM D — GENERAL TRAINING DISTURBANCE CONTROL

**Mandatory. XVIII omitted this control.**

A model may develop a different organization because the intervention disrupts optimization broadly.

That alternative must be tested directly.

## D1. Construct an independently motivated control

Choose a non-targeted perturbation that creates a comparable training disturbance without intentionally removing the relevant relational computation.

Possible matching quantities include:

- Immediate training-loss increase.
- Cumulative training-loss disturbance.
- Gradient norm.
- Parameter-update magnitude.
- Optimizer-state displacement.
- Activation perturbation.

These quantities need not agree.

Predeclare which matching measure is relevant to each causal explanation.

## D2. Validate non-targeted status

The control cannot be called non-targeted simply because its attention indices differ from the original mask.

XVIII found that part of the earlier off-route sham overlapped with the targeted route.

Verify:

- Edge disjointness where intended.
- Lack of dependence on the targeted relational variable.
- Effects on the same parameter groups.
- Effects on complementary routes.
- Whether the control itself alters task-relevant information.

## D3. Reject weak controls

A control that produces negligible disruption cannot rule out a disturbance-based explanation for a strong intervention.

Likewise, one that destroys overall task competence is not a meaningful control for selecting between competent strategies.

If a properly matched control cannot be constructed, leave the general-disturbance explanation unresolved.

Do not replace the missing control with a convenient but irrelevant perturbation.

# 8. WORKSTREAM E — INDEPENDENT FUNCTIONAL AND CAUSAL VALIDATION

**Mandatory.**

An extremely important ambiguity remains from XIII–XVIII.

**Changing which pathway is necessary does not necessarily mean changing which mathematical computation the network performs.**

XIX must distinguish the two.

## E1. Separate three claims

**Claim 1 — Route reorganization**

The same or similar computation is carried through different neural components.

**Claim 2 — Different functional computation**

The model uses a different input-output rule or evidence-combination function.

**Claim 3 — Different internal algorithm**

The model implements a genuinely different computational procedure, beyond mere anatomical routing.

These claims require progressively stronger evidence.

Do not use them interchangeably.

## E2. Independent behavioral tests

Construct mathematically valid inputs and interventions that can distinguish the candidate functional computations.

Use the exact T4 reference mathematics.

Include:

- Changes in source reliability.
- Controlled source agreement and disagreement.
- Relevant counterfactual evidence edits.
- Cases where previously fitted organizational descriptions make different predictions.
- Appropriate on-support and explicitly labeled off-support cases.

A fitting parameter that is non-unique cannot establish a unique neural operator.

## E3. Independent causal tests

Use genuine test-time causal interventions to determine which components are necessary or sufficient.

Avoid using only the same attention edges that were manipulated during training.

Where those edges must be used, include independent functional evidence and acknowledge the potential measurement dependence.

## E4. Competence and uncertainty

Report:

- Bayes-reference task performance.
- Held-out accuracy and NLL.
- Competence of every trained model.
- Functional classification.
- Causal-route classification.
- Agreement or disagreement between classifications.

A model can have a changed causal route without having changed its mathematical decision function.

If that is what the evidence shows, report **causal implementation reorganization** rather than algorithm selection.

Do not force hybrid models into early-versus-mixture binary classes.

# 10. SCIENTIFIC IDENTIFICATION STANDARD

XIX must explicitly answer what it would mean to isolate a mechanism.

A single successful intervention is not enough.

At minimum, a strong causal result should establish:

1. A precisely defined intervention.
2. Verified forward and backward effects.
3. A measurable effect on later organization.
4. Appropriate negative controls.
5. A competing causal explanation that predicts something different.
6. A reciprocal or complementary intervention where meaningful.
7. An independent organizational diagnostic.
8. Competent resulting models.
9. Robustness across independent experimental units.
10. A clear boundary on what is and is not identified.

Where parameter interactions make a unique explanation impossible, document the interaction.

An identified **combination** of necessary changes is scientifically acceptable.

Do not invent a single dominant cause if several components jointly mediate the effect.

# 11. FROZEN PREDICTIONS

**Freeze only genuinely discriminating predictions.**

Suggested prediction families:

### P1 — Parameter-state mediation

A specified component of the intervention-trained state changes the later organization when transplanted into a baseline trajectory, beyond transplant controls.

### P2 — Reciprocal necessity

Restoring the corresponding baseline component in the intervened trajectory prevents or meaningfully reduces the organizational change.

### P3 — Optimizer-history contribution

Optimizer-state transplantation produces a quantitatively distinguishable result from parameter-only transplantation.

### P4 — Forward versus value-learning contribution

The newly isolated value-gradient comparison distinguishes at least two causal explanations of XVIII's content-destruction result.

### P5 — Routing-gradient contribution

A graph-verified routing-learning intervention has a distinct predicted effect from a merely local logit-gradient stop.

### P6 — Non-targeted disturbance

A genuinely comparable non-targeted intervention does or does not reproduce the organizational change under preregistered conditions.

### P7 — Functional versus routing change

A specific intervention changes causal pathway necessity without changing the tested functional decision computation, or changes both, according to a frozen prediction.

These are candidate prediction families, not a quota.

Reject any prediction whose expected outcome is mathematically guaranteed, already known from development, or incapable of distinguishing explanations.

Do not report multiple rescored versions of the same causal observation as independent confirmations.
